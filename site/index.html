Neural operators are fast but struggle with complicated shapes. Domain decomposition turns a complicated shape into simple pieces. How it works

Cut. Solve. Stitch.

One real case from the demo: heat flowing along a steel channel shaped like an S. Nothing here is a mock-up. The shape, the pieces and the temperatures are the demo's own. The same three steps hold at any level of detail: cut finer where it matters, coarser where it does not.

a piece a seam cold to hot

Cut

Cut it where it bends.

The channel is cut into 4 pieces that follow its shape, not boxes. Where two pieces overlap there is a seam: 3 of them here.

Solve

Every piece gets its own expert.

Each piece is solved by its own expert: a learned operator, a classical solver, or a group of smaller experts working as one. In the demo today the experts are classical. Swapping one kind for another changes nothing around it.

Stitch

Seams carry power, and power adds up.

The pieces trade what crosses their seams (here, heat) until they agree. Nothing is lost at a seam. The assembled temperatures match the channel solved whole to within 1.8×10−9 of the temperature span.

Checked before it runs

Every connection is read before anything runs.

A compiler reads only what each piece declares: force, rotation, heat, current or flow, each an effort and a flow whose product is power. It admits each connection, admits it without a certificate, or refuses it, and cites the rule for every decision. A new case is declarations, not code.

Half one · Domain decomposition

The power of domain decomposition.

A complicated shape is hard to solve in one piece: a single grid has to bend around every curve, and a single solver works at the pace of its hardest corner. Cut the shape into simple pieces and each piece is small, simply shaped and solved alongside the others. As the problem grows more complicated, the advantage grows.

Same answer, nothing lost at a seam. Where the pieces iterate to agreement, they land within 3.6×10−9 of the undivided answer, as a share of the field's scale, and 34 of 34 balance checks (mass, energy, charge and force) pass.

Speeds are ratios: the time of the whole domain solved at once, divided by the time of the pieces, same grid, same machine. Measured on the owner's Windows 11 laptop, 22 logical processors, on mains power, no other Python process.

The Atlas workbench

Race the pieces yourself.

The workbench runs on your own machine. Choose a kind of physics, draw the domain, cut it into pieces, and race the pieces against the undivided solve. Change the shape or the cut and run again. Nothing is sent anywhere.

In this project

Our first learned expert.

In the workbench's wind farm, a small network trained on 100 random layouts steps every piece. Inside the same pieces it ran 1.52× faster than the classical expert it replaces, at the same error (farm power 7.50% from the reference, against 7.53%), and 7.45× faster than the farm solved whole.

It did not clear its own gate. A classical solver on a grid twice as coarse came closer (3.63%) in about a third of the time, so 5 of the six bars set before training passed. It is a first step; the strong evidence for speed is the published work above.

One evaluation, on the laptop on mains power. The record.

Putting the halves together

A universally compatible neural operator: the chart operator.

Decomposition turns a complicated shape into simple pieces. Learned experts solve simple problems fast. Together, learned experts can solve decomposed problems much faster than either half alone, provided each expert is built to be a piece.

Why today's neural operators aren't built for the job

Most learn a whole problem for one family of shapes: fast inside that family, hard to reuse on new domains (Secchi et al.). The core obstacle is that a trained operator does not transfer to new geometries (Huang et al.). A piece also has to take what its neighbours tell it at the seams, keep pace with the coupling, and be checkable. When we placed a public pretrained neural operator inside Atlas, twelve such properties were missing.

Others have started joining the halves

Learned pieces assembled by Schwarz iteration already generalise to new shapes and to domains far larger than they were trained on. The cards below quote them. Atlas adds one contract that every kind of expert plugs into, with its central guarantees proved.

A different formulation.

A traditional neural operator predicts a field over a whole domain. The chart operator predicts how one piece responds at its joints. A chart maps the piece onto a square, a learned operator solves the square, and the piece reports its stiffness at its joints, the way structural engineers assemble a truss.

Charts for geometry

Every piece is mapped onto a square by a map that is checked never to fold. The curved piece becomes a square, with its geometry passed in as data, so one operator can serve many shapes.

Superelements for agreement

Each piece reports only its stiffness at its joints. The host assembles the joints and solves them once, with no back-and-forth. Any expert that reports its joints this way can sit beside it.

Operators for physics

A learned operator solves one problem on the square, given the chart's geometry and the values on its four sides, and returns the field.

Compatible by design

  • Any expert. A piece can be a learned operator, a classical solver, or a group of smaller experts behind one set of joints. The rest of the system sees only the joints.
  • Any level of detail. Change the geometry or the cut to make a simulation finer where detail matters and broader where it does not. The experts and the contract stay the same.
  • Any power-carrying seam. Force, heat, current and flow each cross a seam as an effort and a flow whose product is power. That is one contract for every kind of physics, and the compiler checks each connection before anything runs.

Built this way, a learned piece cannot break the assembly. Whatever the network returns, the assembled system can be solved, and its answer is the best its pieces can represent. That is proved in Lean, as Atlas.tier0_headline. Errors in a piece enter squared: with every field off by 10%, the joints came out off by 0.9%, and every piece stayed well behaved. Predicting the joints directly was off by 10%, and 12 of 16 pieces broke.

A design, not a build: no chart operator has been trained. The errors above were put in by hand to test the assembly, not produced by a trained network. The record.

Atlas.defect_correction_headline

The proofs changed the design.

One statement we planned to prove, T3, is false as first written for one standard method, restricted additive Schwarz. Lean would not accept it, and a small counterexample shows why. The corrected statement needs a sweep that converges, and the framework's compiler now requires exactly that before it admits such a coupling. The proof did not certify the design after the fact. It changed it.

The counterexample

Checked by machine, line by line.

A clean re-check of the whole project takes 100 s on a laptop on battery. We searched for an earlier machine-checked theory of domain decomposition and found none; machine-checked proofs of related iterations do exist.

The vision

The full vision.

Vision. Not built yet.

Imagine a full system, decomposed into its individual parts. Every part is an expert, and every expert (like the power unit or the radiators) can be a neural operator, a classical model, or itself made of smaller experts inside. Experts work on their own time and length scales and their own physics, and still agree through one standard, checked interface that carries force, heat, current or flow. The physics is the first instance; the framework is built to generalise to real engineering systems.

Three scenes at three scales. The pictures are illustrations; the graphs on them are drawn from each scenario's design.

Illustration

The whole race car, assembled.

A race car is air, heat, rubber, metal and electricity at once. Today each is simulated by its own tool and stitched together by hand. Atlas would give each part its own expert, let them talk through the forces and heat they share, and solve the whole car together, fast enough to try a design in minutes.

Today: a 2-D car cut into pieces on a rolling road, and a 3-D half-car in a box, both classical; a vehicle of many agents across five families of physics, with a rotor, a radiator and a powertrain circuit. A learned piece at the car's pace was too slow and left its range. That is the missing piece.

Illustration

A wildfire, forecast and steered.

A wildfire is fire, fuel, terrain and wind feeding each other. Atlas would split the landscape into pieces that follow its ridges and valleys, give each an expert for how fire spreads through its fuel, and let the pieces pass heat, embers and wind across their edges. The aim: fast enough to forecast hours ahead, and to test where a firebreak or an air drop would do the most good before anyone is sent.

Today: nothing fire-specific is built. The nearest pieces are the demo's pollutant plume carried by a solved flow and its flow over drawn terrain. This scenario is the furthest from today's record.

Illustration

The agentic Moon base.

A Moon base is a machine the size of a town where every system leans on the others. A landing blasts dust at the solar arrays; the arrays feed the batteries; the batteries keep the habitat warm through the two-week night; the radiators shed the heat. Atlas would give each system its own agent. The solar towers would not be one network: they would be one agent, trained as an operator, written as a mathematical model, or grouped from smaller experts, running on its own resources and its own clock. Every cable, pipe and plume would become a typed conversation that carries power, heat or force, each one checked.

Today: a seven-agent rocket ascent in 2-D, coupled and conserving; a powertrain from rotor shaft to battery; a closed coolant loop; and fields coupled to lumped circuits in the demo. Plumes on regolith and the lunar environment are not built.
