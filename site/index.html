How it works

Cut. Solve. Stitch.

One real case from the demo: heat flowing along a steel channel shaped like an S. Nothing here is a mock-up. The shape, the pieces and the temperatures are the demo's own.

a piece a seam cold to hot

Cut

Cut it where it bends.

The channel is cut into 4 pieces that follow its shape, not boxes. Where two pieces overlap there is a seam: 3 of them here.

Solve

Every piece gets its own expert.

Each piece is solved by its own solver. Today that expert is a classical solver. Next it is a learned one, built to be a piece.

Stitch

Seams carry power, and power adds up.

The pieces trade what crosses their seams (here, heat) until they agree. Nothing is lost at a seam. The assembled temperatures match the channel solved whole to within 1.8×10−9 of the temperature span.

Checked before it runs

Every connection is read before anything runs.

A compiler reads only what each piece declares: force, rotation, heat, current or flow, each an effort and a flow whose product is power. It admits each connection, admits it without a certificate, or refuses it, and cites the rule for every decision. A new case is declarations, not code.

The missing piece

Today's AI solvers weren't built to be pieces.

We trained one for the wind farm. Here is what happened.

A small network, trained on 100 random farm layouts and tested on one it had never seen, stepped every piece of a wind farm. Before any data existed we wrote down six bars it had to clear.

It matched the classical pieces: farm power 7.50% from the reference against their 7.53%. It ran 1.52× faster than them and 7.45× faster than the whole domain.

Then a classical solver on a grid twice as coarse came closer to the reference (3.63%) in about a third of the time. The coarse solver won, so the gate failed: 5 of six bars passed.

Errors are farm power against a reference run at twice the resolution, read at macro-step 12 of 40; times are the mean per macro-step. One evaluation, on the laptop on mains power. The record.

The network learned to copy the classical pieces, so it inherited their error. Today's learned models were built for whole, square, periodic problems. Inside a decomposition they fall short in ways we measured, twelve of them.

  • S1✕Accepts the piece's own shape, not one fixed square grid
  • S2✕Runs at the coupling's time step
  • S3✕Takes the data at its seams as an input
  • S4✕Has a bounded reach across a seam
  • S5✕Damps slow modes as the classical solver does
  • S6✕Is never nearer singular than the classical solver
  • S7✕Is cheaper than the cheapest classical alternative
  • S8✕Has a reference of its own class to be checked against
  • S9✕Is accurate enough to be worth its slot
  • S10✕Has the numerical precision the checks need
  • S11✕Rolls forward stably on its own
  • S12✕Carries a licence that allows its use

Each property was measured missing in a public pretrained neural operator used inside the Atlas framework.

The answer: the chart operator.

A learned expert designed to be a piece. Charts for geometry. Superelements for agreement. Operators for physics.

Charts for geometry

Every piece is mapped onto a square by a map that is checked never to fold. The curved piece becomes a square with its geometry passed in as data.

Superelements for agreement

Each piece reports its stiffness at its joints, the way structural engineers assemble a truss. The host assembles the joints and solves them once, with no back-and-forth.

Operators for physics

A learned operator solves one problem on the square, given the chart's geometry and the values on its four sides, and returns the field.

Built this way, a learned piece cannot break the assembly: whatever the network returns, the assembled system can be solved, and its answer is the best its pieces can represent. Errors in a piece enter squared. With every field off by 10%, the joints came out off by 0.9%, and every piece stayed well behaved. Predicting the joints directly was off by 10%, and 12 of 16 pieces broke.

A design, not a build: no chart operator has been trained. The errors above were put in by hand to test the assembly, not produced by a trained network. The record.

The checker caught a mistake in our own plan.

One statement we planned to prove, T3, is false as first written for one standard method, restricted additive Schwarz. Lean would not accept it, and a small counterexample shows why. The corrected statement needs a sweep that converges, and that is what the framework's compiler now requires before it admits such a coupling.

The counterexample

Checked by machine, line by line.

A clean re-check of the whole project takes 100 s on a laptop on battery. We searched for an earlier machine-checked theory of domain decomposition and found none; machine-checked proofs of related iterations do exist.

The vision

What it unlocks.

Vision. Not built yet.

Three scenes at three scales. In each, every part is an expert, and every link between them is a typed conversation that carries force, heat, current or flow, and is checked before anything runs. The pictures are illustrations; the graphs on them are drawn from each scenario's design.

The whole race car, assembled.

A race car is air, heat, rubber, metal and electricity at once. Today each is simulated by its own tool and stitched together by hand. Atlas would give each part its own expert, let them talk through the forces and heat they share, and solve the whole car together, fast enough to try a design in minutes.

A learned piece at the car's pace was too slow and left its range. That is the missing piece.

A wildfire, forecast and steered.

A wildfire is fire, fuel, terrain and wind feeding each other. Atlas would split the landscape into pieces that follow its ridges and valleys, give each an expert for how fire spreads through its fuel, and let the pieces pass heat, embers and wind across their edges. The agentic Moon base.

A Moon base is a machine the size of a town where every system leans on the others. A landing blasts dust at the solar arrays; the arrays feed the batteries; the batteries keep the habitat warm through the two-week night; the radiators shed the heat. Today: a seven-agent rocket ascent in 2-D, coupled and conserving; a powertrain from rotor shaft to battery; a closed coolant loop; and fields coupled to lumped circuits in the demo. Plumes on regolith and the lunar environment are not built.

The path

From a square of heat to a whole world.

Four stages, each with what it must prove and the gate it must pass first. The first gate is the number of iterations, before any claim of speed.

Stage 0

Heat diffusing through pieces of random shape: geometry through the chart, the learned piece's output, the assembly at the joints, and its cost against the classical alternatives.

Gate: on the demo's own drawn examples, the bend and the S-channel among them.

Stage 1

Flow carrying a substance, elastic structures and electric current: the same contract across port types, and a seam between two physics with a learned expert on each side.

Gate: per family, plus the cooled block's seam.

Stage 2

Fluid flow, the first nonlinear family; the demo's learned wind farm, generalised from rectangles to curved pieces; and a controlled comparison of training from scratch against fine-tuning a pretrained model.

Gate: on the wind farm and a flow past a cylinder.

Stage 3

Three dimensions, and compressible and reacting flow: the scale of the vision's car, fire front and plume on regolith.

Gate: set after Stage 2.

No cost is shown: the training cost has not been measured yet, and estimates stay off this page.
