Chart Operators: Learned Superelements for Domain Decomposition on Curved Geometry

Naunidh Singh
Draft of 1 October 2026

Research proposal. No network has been trained. Every number is either measured by a named script, with classical solvers or untrained networks, or labelled as an estimate.

Abstract

Domain decomposition solves a boundary value problem by dividing the domain into subdomains, solving on each subdomain separately, and enforcing agreement of the solution and of its flux across the shared boundaries. Replacing the subdomain solver by a trained neural network is attractive when many similar subdomain problems must be solved, but the networks available today accept only rectangular grids, take no boundary data as input, and, as measured in this project, can stall the iteration that brings the subdomains into agreement. We propose the chart operator, a learned subdomain solver designed for this setting. Topology is assigned to the decomposition, which cuts the domain into four-sided pieces. Geometry is assigned to a classical map of each piece onto the unit square, accompanied by an exact and inexpensive certificate of injectivity; the map turns every piece into a diffusion problem with an anisotropic coefficient on the square, and the network solves only that problem. Its output is a set of constraint modes: for each interface mode, the field inside the piece whose boundary values equal that mode. The coupling software, not the network, forms each piece's interface stiffness matrix as the energy Gram matrix of these modes. We prove that this matrix is symmetric positive semidefinite for every network output; that its error is the Gram matrix of the field errors, hence quadratic in them and of one sign; that the coupled system is uniquely solvable whenever the exact one is; and that the coupled solution is the energy-optimal approximation in the span of the learned modes. A corollary is a training objective that needs no solved examples and has the same gradients as supervised training in the energy norm. Measurements with classical solvers in place of the network show that the Gram construction keeps the coupled system positive definite where a network predicting the interface matrix directly does not, and that it removes the coupling iteration altogether, where the classical Schwarz methods we measured need between 70 and 1,864 rounds of subdomain solves on 64 pieces. We report the forward cost of candidate networks, a training plan, and an evaluation protocol registered before any training.

1Introduction

Many simulations in engineering are posed on domains that are large, irregular in shape, and assembled from parts of different materials: a cooling channel that bends, a bracket with rounded fillets, a heat exchanger with many similar passages. Domain decomposition divides such a domain into subdomains, solves the governing equations on each subdomain with boundary data supplied by its neighbours, and enforces agreement of the subdomain solutions across the shared boundaries [1, 2]. It underlies most parallel solvers for partial differential equations, because each subdomain problem is small and the subdomains can be solved at the same time.

A trained network that approximates the solution operator of the subdomain problem, a neural operator [3, 4], could take the place of the classical subdomain solver. One evaluation of such a network costs a fixed number of arithmetic operations whatever the conditioning of the problem, and one trained network can serve every subdomain of a given kind. Several recent works compose learned subdomain solvers by Schwarz iteration; Section 9 reviews them.

This proposal is motivated by a measurement campaign in which a pretrained network was used as the subdomain solver inside coupled simulations (Section 3). The campaign recorded twelve specific shortcomings. Three of them determine the present design. The network accepted only a uniform rectangular grid, so that 61% of the unknowns of a curved-geometry test case could not be assigned to it at all. It took no boundary data as an input: the values of its neighbours had to be written into a border of grid cells that it had not been trained to read. And its response to a change in boundary data differed from that of the classical solver on exactly the slowly converging error components that determine the number of coupling iterations.

We propose a learned subdomain solver, the chart operator, built on four separations of responsibility:

topology $\to$ the decomposition,    geometry $\to$ a classical chart,    physics $\to$ the learned operator,    agreement $\to$ an assembly by the host.

The decomposition cuts the domain into pieces that each have four sides, so the network never encounters a hole or a junction (Section 4.1). Each piece is mapped onto the unit square by a classical map, a chart, whose injectivity is checked exactly. A change of variables turns the curved piece into the unit square carrying an anisotropic diffusion coefficient, so the network receives the geometry as coefficient fields on a fixed grid (Section 4.2). The network solves one problem only, diffusion on the unit square with prescribed values on its four sides, and returns its answer as constraint modes (Section 4.3). The host, the software that owns the coupling, computes from these modes the interface stiffness of each piece and solves one interface system; it performs no iteration (Section 5). The name follows differential geometry, in which a manifold is described by an atlas of charts, each a map between a flat reference domain and one patch.

The guarantee we prove concerns the coupling, not the accuracy of the network. Whatever the network returns, the coupled system is uniquely solvable, no piece can appear softer than it is, and the coupled solution is the best that the network's modes allow in the energy norm. Accuracy remains an empirical question, to be answered by the protocol of Section 8. We also report a negative result. At the two-dimensional problem sizes measured here, a single sparse direct solve of the whole problem is faster than every decomposed method we tried, classical or learned. The case for a learned subdomain solver rests on settings in which classical work cannot be reused or does not scale, which Section 7 identifies.

2Background

This section defines every method on which the design depends. It assumes linear algebra, multivariable calculus, real analysis and the theory of second-order elliptic equations, and it assumes no prior knowledge of domain decomposition or of machine learning. All results are stated for the discretised problem, so every space is finite-dimensional; the theorems of Section 5 are proved in that setting.

2.1The model problem and its energy

Let $\Omega\subset\R^2$ be a bounded Lipschitz domain and let $\kappa$ be a conductivity: a function on $\Omega$ whose values are symmetric $2\times2$ matrices, uniformly positive definite and bounded. The model problem is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:model} \sigma u-\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla u)=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=g\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega, \end{equation}

with $\sigma\ge0$. For $\sigma=0$ it is steady heat conduction. For $\sigma=c/\Delta t$ it is one step of the backward (implicit) Euler method applied to $c\,\partial_tu-\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla u)=s$, with $c$ the heat capacity, $\Delta t$ the time step and $f=s+c\,u^{\text{old}}/\Delta t$. The Fourier number of a step on a region of diameter $L$,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:fourier} \Fo=\frac{\kappa\,\Delta t}{c\,L^{2}}, \end{equation}

is the dimensionless step size: heat diffuses a distance of order $\sqrt{\kappa\Delta t/c}$ in one step, so $\Fo$ is the square of that distance relative to the size of the region. For $\Fo\ll1$ a step only alters the solution in a thin layer next to any change in the data; for $\Fo\gg1$ a step behaves like a steady solve.

The energy of a function $v$ is $a(v,v)$, where

\begin{equation}\label{eq:energy} a(v,w)=\int_\Omega\bigl(\kappa\nabla v\cdot\nabla w+\sigma\,v\,w\bigr)\,dx,\qquad \lVert v\rVert_a=a(v,v)^{1/2}. \end{equation}

A weak solution of \eqref{eq:model} satisfies $a(u,w)=\int_\Omega fw\,dx$ for every $w$ that vanishes on $\partial\Omega$. The Dirichlet principle characterises it variationally: among all functions with the boundary values $g$, the solution minimises $\tfrac12a(v,v)-\int_\Omega fv\,dx$. In particular, for $f=0$ the solution is the function of least energy with the given boundary values, and it is $a$-orthogonal to every function that vanishes on the boundary.

We discretise by cell-centred finite volumes on a quadrilateral grid. The unknowns are the values of $u$ in the cells; the flux between two neighbouring cells is a face conductance times the difference of their values, with the conductance the harmonic mean of the cells' conductivities. The resulting matrix $A$ is symmetric and positive definite, and $v^\top Av$ is the discrete energy. Partitioning the unknowns into interior ones (index $I$) and boundary ones (index $\Gamma$), the discrete Dirichlet principle reads

\begin{equation}\label{eq:schur} \min_{v_I}\begin{pmatrix}v_I\\ g\end{pmatrix}^{\!\top}\!\begin{pmatrix}A_{II} & A_{I\Gamma}\\ A_{\Gamma I} & A_{\Gamma\Gamma}\end{pmatrix}\!\begin{pmatrix}v_I\\ g\end{pmatrix}=g^\top Sg,\qquad S=A_{\Gamma\Gamma}-A_{\Gamma I}A_{II}^{-1}A_{I\Gamma}, \end{equation}

with the minimum attained at $v_I=-A_{II}^{-1}A_{I\Gamma}\,g$. The matrix $S$ is the Schur complement of $A_{II}$; it is symmetric and positive semidefinite, because $g^\top Sg$ is a minimum of a nonnegative quadratic form.

2.2Domain decomposition

Divide $\Omega$ into non-overlapping subdomains $\Omega_1,\dots,\Omega_P$, which we call pieces. The interface $\Gamma$ is the union of the boundaries that pieces share. A function assembled from solutions $u_i$ on the pieces solves \eqref{eq:model} exactly when, on each shared boundary $\Gamma_{ij}$, the two transmission conditions hold:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:transmission} u_i=u_j,\qquad (\kappa\nabla u_i)\cdot n_i+(\kappa\nabla u_j)\cdot n_j=0\qquad\text{on }\Gamma_{ij}, \end{equation}

where $n_i$ is the outward unit normal of $\Omega_i$. The first condition is continuity of temperature, the second continuity of heat flux.

The Dirichlet-to-Neumann map $\Lambda_i$ of a piece takes boundary values $\lambda$ on $\partial\Omega_i$, solves $\sigma w-\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla w)=0$ in $\Omega_i$ with $w=\lambda$ on $\partial\Omega_i$, and returns the normal flux $(\kappa\nabla w)\cdot n_i$ on $\partial\Omega_i$. By Green's identity, $\langle\Lambda_i\lambda,\nu\rangle=a_i(w_\lambda,w_\nu)$, where $w_\lambda$ and $w_\nu$ are the solutions with boundary values $\lambda$ and $\nu$. Hence $\Lambda_i$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite. For $\sigma=0$ its null space consists of the constants, since a uniform temperature carries no flux. In the discrete setting $\Lambda_i$ is the Schur complement $S_i$ of \eqref{eq:schur} for the piece; it is the stiffness of the piece as seen from its boundary.

Take as unknown the common value $\lambda$ of the solution on $\Gamma$. Given $\lambda$, each piece's solution is determined by one subdomain solve, and the second transmission condition becomes a linear system for $\lambda$ alone:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:interface} \Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{P}R_i^\top\Lambda_iR_i\Bigr)\lambda=\chi, \end{equation}

where $R_i$ restricts interface values to $\partial\Omega_i\cap\Gamma$ and $\chi$ collects the flux imbalance produced by $f$ and $g$ when $\lambda=0$. The matrix of \eqref{eq:interface} is the interface Schur complement (also called the Steklov–Poincaré operator). It is symmetric, and positive definite whenever the global problem has a unique solution. Solving \eqref{eq:interface} directly and then recovering each piece's interior by one more subdomain solve is called substructuring or static condensation: the interior unknowns of every piece are condensed out, and only the interface is solved globally.

2.3Iterative coupling: Schwarz methods and their acceleration

Forming $\Lambda_i$ costs one subdomain solve per interface unknown. Iterative coupling methods avoid this: they only ever solve subdomain problems with given boundary data, and repeat until the transmission conditions hold. We call one solve on every piece a round.

Schwarz methods. The alternating method of Schwarz [5] uses overlapping subdomains. It solves on the first subdomain with boundary values taken from the current approximation on the second, then on the second with values from the first, and repeats; the error contracts in each round by a factor that depends on the width of the overlap relative to the size of the subdomains. The additive variant solves all subdomains simultaneously from the previous approximation, which suits parallel computers; the multiplicative variant solves them in sequence, each using the latest values. Restricted additive Schwarz (RAS) is the additive variant in common use [2]. These methods exchange values of the solution only, a Dirichlet exchange.

The diameter bound. In one round, information moves from a piece only to its neighbours. For a steady problem the solution at each point depends on the data everywhere, so any scheme in which pieces communicate only with neighbours needs at least $D$ rounds, where $D$ is the number of pieces across the domain. For this reason the round counts of such one-level methods grow with the number of pieces.

Robin exchange and optimised Schwarz methods. Without overlap, a Dirichlet exchange does not converge. Lions [6] showed that non-overlapping pieces converge when they exchange a combination of value and flux. In round $r+1$, piece $i$ solves with the Robin boundary condition

\begin{equation}\label{eq:robin} (\kappa\nabla u_i^{r+1})\cdot n_i+p\,u_i^{r+1}=-(\kappa\nabla u_j^{r})\cdot n_j+p\,u_j^{r}\qquad\text{on }\Gamma_{ij}, \end{equation}

with a parameter $p>0$; at a fixed point both transmission conditions \eqref{eq:transmission} hold. Optimised Schwarz methods choose $p$ to make the iteration converge fastest [7]. For the Laplacian on two half-planes, an error component that oscillates along the interface with wavenumber $k$ is multiplied in one exchange there and back by

\begin{equation}\label{eq:osm-rate} \rho(k,p)=\left(\frac{p-|k|}{p+|k|}\right)^{2}, \end{equation}

with $|k|$ replaced by $\sqrt{k^2+\sigma/\kappa}$ when $\sigma>0$. On a grid of spacing $h$ in pieces of size $L$, the wavenumbers that occur lie between $\pi/L$ and $\pi/h$; the choice $p^\star=\sqrt{(\pi/L)(\pi/h)}$ minimises the largest factor, which is then $1-O(h^{1/2})$, against $1-O(h)$ for the classical method with an overlap of one cell [7].

Coarse spaces. Error components that vary slowly across many pieces are the ones the diameter bound protects. A two-level method adds, in each round, a correction computed from a small global problem posed on a few functions per piece, for instance the functions $1,\xi,\eta,\xi\eta$ in the coordinates of each piece. With a suitable coarse space the number of rounds becomes almost independent of the number of pieces [1].

Krylov and Anderson acceleration. For a linear problem, one round is an affine map $x\mapsto Tx+c$ on the interface data, and the limit solves $(I-T)x=c$. The generalised minimal residual method (GMRES) [8] builds the Krylov subspace spanned by $r_0,(I-T)r_0,(I-T)^2r_0,\dots$, where $r_0$ is the initial residual, and selects at each step the element of $x_0$ plus this subspace with the smallest residual. Its convergence depends on how the eigenvalues of $I-T$ cluster, not only on the spectral radius of $T$, so it is usually much faster than repeating the round. Applying $I-T$ to a vector costs one round when the subdomain solver is linear; when it is nonlinear or a black box, it requires Jacobian–vector products, that is, directional derivatives of the solver. Anderson acceleration [9, 10] needs no derivatives: it forms the next iterate as an affine combination of the last few iterates, with weights that minimise the norm of the correspondingly combined residuals.

2.4Substructuring with reduced interfaces

Static condensation becomes inexpensive when the interface values are restricted to a few modes. On each side of a piece, parametrised by $s\in[0,1]$, we take as port modes the cosines $q_k(s)=\cos(k\pi s)$, $k=0,\dots,m-1$, sampled at the boundary faces and orthonormalised; collected over the four sides they form a matrix $Q$ with $4m$ columns. The interface value on a side is $\lambda=Qc$, with $c$ the vector of port coordinates. Cosines are the eigenfunctions of the one-dimensional Laplacian with zero end-derivatives, so the truncation error of a smooth function decreases rapidly with $m$.

The constraint mode $H_k$ of a piece is the solution in its interior with boundary values equal to the $k$-th port mode and zero source: the response of the piece to a unit amount of mode $k$ with every other mode held at zero. The term comes from structural dynamics, where Craig and Bampton [11] used such modes to couple substructures of aircraft. The same objects appear as the basis functions of the multiscale finite element method [12]. The port matrix of the piece is its Dirichlet-to-Neumann map restricted to the port modes,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:portmatrix} \Lambda^{(m)}=Q^\top S\,Q\in\R^{4m\times4m}, \end{equation}

and a piece represented only by its port matrix and a load vector, assembled into a global system as if it were a single finite element, is called a superelement. Reduced-basis methods for components connected through such reduced ports are developed in [13, 14].

The trade-off with iteration is the following. Forming $\Lambda^{(m)}$ costs $4m$ subdomain solves per piece. Afterwards one interface system with $m$ unknowns per shared side is solved, each piece's interior is reconstructed from its modes, and no iteration takes place. When the pieces do not change, the port matrices are formed once and reused for every subsequent solve.

2.5Maps between domains

Let $\hat\Omega=[0,1]^2$ be the reference square with coordinates $(\xi,\eta)$. A chart of a piece $\Omega_i$ is a continuous bijection $F:\hat\Omega\to\overline{\Omega_i}$ that is continuously differentiable on the open square and orientation-preserving, with Jacobian determinant $J=\det DF>0$. It carries the four sides of the square onto the four sides of the piece.

The pull-back. For functions $u,v$ on $\Omega_i$, write $\hat u=u\circ F$ and $\hat v=v\circ F$. The chain rule gives $\nabla u\circ F=DF^{-\top}\hat\nabla\hat u$, and the change of variables gives $dx=J\,d\hat x$, so

\begin{equation}\label{eq:pullback} \int_{\Omega_i}\bigl(\kappa\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+\sigma uv\bigr)dx=\int_{\hat\Omega}\bigl(\mathsf A\,\hat\nabla\hat u\cdot\hat\nabla\hat v+J\hat\sigma\,\hat u\hat v\bigr)d\hat x,\qquad \mathsf A=J\,DF^{-1}\,\hat\kappa\,DF^{-\top}, \end{equation}

with $\hat\kappa=\kappa\circ F$ and $\hat\sigma=\sigma\circ F$. The problem on the curved piece is therefore the same problem on the square, with the conductivity replaced by the matrix field $\mathsf A$, the capacity term multiplied by $J$, and the source multiplied by $J$. The flux through any part of the boundary is the same number in both coordinate systems, so the transmission conditions can be imposed in either. A consequence noted by Kohn and Vogelius [15] is that a piece is characterised from its boundary only through $\mathsf A$: two pieces of different shape whose pulled-back coefficients agree have the same Dirichlet-to-Neumann map once their boundaries are identified through the charts. Seen from the boundary, geometry and anisotropy are indistinguishable.

The Beltrami coefficient. Identify $\R^2$ with $\mathbb C$, write $z=\xi+i\eta$, and regard $F$ as a complex-valued function with derivatives $F_z=\tfrac12(F_\xi-iF_\eta)$ and $F_{\bar z}=\tfrac12(F_\xi+iF_\eta)$. The Beltrami coefficient and the dilatation of $F$ are

\begin{equation}\label{eq:beltrami} \mu=\frac{F_{\bar z}}{F_z},\qquad K=\frac{1+|\mu|}{1-|\mu|}. \end{equation}

One has $J=|F_z|^2(1-|\mu|^2)$, so $F$ preserves orientation with $J>0$ exactly where $|\mu|<1$. The dilatation is the ratio of the largest to the smallest singular value of $DF$: an infinitesimal circle is mapped to an ellipse with axis ratio $K$ [16]. A map with $\mu=0$ is conformal: it preserves angles. With $G=DF^\top DF$ the metric of the chart, a computation given in Appendix B shows

\begin{equation}\label{eq:metric-mu} J\,G^{-1}=\frac{1}{1-|\mu|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}|1-\mu|^{2} & -2\operatorname{Im}\mu\\ -2\operatorname{Im}\mu & |1+\mu|^{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det\bigl(J\,G^{-1}\bigr)=1. \end{equation}

For a scalar conductivity, $\mathsf A=\hat\kappa\,J\,G^{-1}$, so the coefficient of the pulled-back problem is determined by $\hat\kappa$ and $\mu$ alone; $J$ enters only through the capacity and the source.

Conformal modulus. A quadrilateral is a Jordan domain with four marked boundary points. For every quadrilateral there is a unique $M>0$, its conformal modulus, such that the quadrilateral is mapped conformally onto the rectangle $[0,M]\times[0,1]$ with the marked points going to the corners [16]. Composing with the scaling $(\xi,\eta)\mapsto(M\xi,\eta)$ gives a chart from the unit square whose Beltrami coefficient is the constant $(M-1)/(M+1)$ and for which $J\,G^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}(1/M,\,M)$. A piece of modulus near $1$ therefore has a conformal chart with $\mathsf A\approx\hat\kappa I$.

Harmonic and interpolated charts. Winslow [17] constructs a chart by solving Laplace's equation for $\xi$ and for $\eta$ on the physical piece, with boundary values that distribute $[0,1]$ along each side, for example in proportion to arc length, and inverting the resulting map. The Radó–Kneser–Choquet theorem guarantees the result: a harmonic map from a Jordan domain onto a convex domain whose boundary correspondence is a homeomorphism is a diffeomorphism of the interiors [18]. The guarantee does not extend to three dimensions, where harmonic maps onto convex bodies can fail to be injective [19]. Transfinite interpolation [20] constructs a chart explicitly from the four side curves $\mathbf a,\mathbf b,\mathbf c,\mathbf d$ (left, bottom, right, top):

\begin{equation}\label{eq:tfi} \begin{aligned}F(\xi,\eta)={}&(1-\xi)\,\mathbf a(\eta)+\xi\,\mathbf c(\eta)+(1-\eta)\,\mathbf b(\xi)+\eta\,\mathbf d(\xi)\\ &-\bigl[(1-\xi)(1-\eta)\mathbf x_{00}+\xi(1-\eta)\mathbf x_{10}+(1-\xi)\eta\,\mathbf x_{01}+\xi\eta\,\mathbf x_{11}\bigr],\end{aligned} \end{equation}

with $\mathbf x_{00},\dots,\mathbf x_{11}$ the corners. It requires no solve, and it carries no guarantee of injectivity.

2.6Neural operators

A neural network is a parametrised function formed by composing affine maps with fixed nonlinear functions applied entry by entry; we use the Gaussian error linear unit, $\operatorname{GELU}(x)=x\,\Phi(x)$ with $\Phi$ the standard normal distribution function [21]. Its parameters $\theta$, the entries of the affine maps, are chosen by training: minimising a loss, an average of a discrepancy over a set of examples, by stochastic gradient descent. One evaluation of a trained network is a forward pass.

A neural operator is a network that maps functions to functions, with its inputs and outputs sampled on a grid, constructed so that the same parameters can be applied at different grid resolutions [3]. The Fourier neural operator [4] applies, in each layer, a learned multiplier to a truncated Fourier series of its input, which implicitly treats the input as periodic. The convolutional neural operator [22] composes convolutions with up- and down-sampling filters that limit the bandwidth of the signal.

A convolution with a $3\times3$ kernel replaces each grid value by a learned weighted sum of its value and its eight neighbours, the same weights at every point. At the edge of a square, the neighbours outside must be supplied by an extension of the function: periodic, even (mirror reflection, which preserves continuity of any function) or odd (reflection with a change of sign, which preserves continuity of a function that vanishes on the edge).

The U-shaped architecture [23] applies blocks of convolutions at successively coarser resolutions, each obtained by averaging $2\times2$ groups of cells, and then at successively finer resolutions, each obtained by interpolation, with skip connections that pass each resolution's features directly across to the corresponding finer stage. Its coarse stages give every output value a dependence on the whole input, which a solution operator of an elliptic problem requires, at a cost proportional to the number of grid cells. Scalar inputs, such as the Fourier number, are introduced by feature-wise linear modulation (FiLM) [24]: every feature channel $h$ is replaced by $(1+\gamma)\,h+\beta$, with $\gamma$ and $\beta$ produced from the scalars by a small learned map.

Pretrained or foundation models are large networks trained on many datasets of solved differential equations, intended to be adapted to new tasks by fine-tuning, that is, by continued training on a small task-specific dataset; examples are Poseidon [25], DPOT [26], Walrus [27] and GPhyT [28]. Low-rank adaptation [29] fine-tunes a large network by adding to each weight matrix a product of two thin matrices and training only those.

3What the record shows

This proposal belongs to a project, Atlas, that builds software for coupling heterogeneous solvers, classical and learned, through typed interfaces, and for certifying the coupled result. Its record contains a long campaign in which a frozen pretrained network, Poseidon-T [25], served as a subdomain solver inside the project's coupled flow solvers. Table 1 lists what the campaign measured. Each row is a property the network lacked, the measurement that showed it, and the place in this paper where the design responds. Three terms recur. A window is a rectangular subdomain assigned to the network. A slow band is the set of error components that the coupling iteration reduces most slowly. Defect correction is an iteration in which the network acts as an approximate inverse and the classical residual corrects its output, so that the network can change the speed of convergence but not the limit.

Two features of the table shape the design. First, three rows (S5, S5a, S6) concern the network's response to perturbations of its input rather than its accuracy: the iteration that couples the pieces converges at a rate set by how each subdomain solver reacts to a change in its boundary data. The design therefore removes that iteration where it can (Section 5.1) and, where it cannot, makes its convergence a checkable property of each network (Section 5.3). Second, rows S1, S3 and S8 are failures of the interface between the network and the coupled solver, not of the network's training; the chart and the constraint-mode format address them by construction.

4Architecture

Figure 1 shows the pipeline. This section describes its parts in the order in which the data flow through them: the decomposition, the chart, the network's output, the network itself, and the conventions for interface data, conservation and time. The coupling is the subject of Section 5.

4.1Topology: decomposition into four-sided pieces

Every piece is a quadrilateral in the sense of Section 2.5: a region bounded by a Jordan curve, with four marked corners that divide its boundary into four sides. Each side is shared with one neighbour or lies on the physical boundary. Topological features of the domain are handled entirely by the decomposition: an annulus is cut into arcs, a T-junction into three channels and a junction piece. The network never sees a hole or a branch. Constructing such block layouts automatically for arbitrary domains is a separate problem and is a build item of this proposal (Section 11).

Within a channel-like block with a start edge, an end edge and two walls, the project's layout software computes two harmonic coordinates. The along coordinate $u$ satisfies $\Delta u=0$, $u=0$ on the start edge, $u=1$ on the end edge and $\partial_nu=0$ on the walls; the across coordinate $v$ satisfies $\Delta v=0$, $v=0$ on one wall, $v=1$ on the other and $\partial_nv=0$ on the two edges. We cut the block along level curves of these coordinates at equally spaced values.

Proposition 1 (Equal-value cuts.)

Let a block have conformal modulus $M$, and let $u,v$ be its along and across coordinates. For integers $n,m\ge1$, $0\le k\lt n$ and $0\le r\lt m$, the piece $\{k/n\le u\le(k+1)/n,\ r/m\le v\le(r+1)/m\}$ is a quadrilateral of modulus $M'=Mm/n$. The map $x\mapsto(nu-k,\ mv-r)$ is a diffeomorphism of the piece onto the open unit square, and the inverse chart $F$ has the constant Beltrami coefficient $\mu=(M'-1)/(M'+1)$, so that $J\,G^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}(1/M',\,M')$. All four corners of an interior piece are right angles.

Proof. Let $\Phi$ be the conformal map of the block onto $[0,M]\times[0,1]$ that sends the start edge to $\{0\}\times[0,1]$ and the walls to the horizontal sides. Then $\operatorname{Re}\Phi$ is harmonic, equals $0$ and $M$ on the two edges, and has zero normal derivative on the walls, because the walls are mapped to horizontal lines on which $\operatorname{Im}\Phi$ is constant and the Cauchy–Riemann equations make $\partial_n\operatorname{Re}\Phi$ proportional to the tangential derivative of $\operatorname{Im}\Phi$. By uniqueness of the solution of this mixed boundary value problem, $\operatorname{Re}\Phi=Mu$; in the same way $\operatorname{Im}\Phi=v$. The piece is therefore the preimage under $\Phi$ of the rectangle $[Mk/n,M(k+1)/n]\times[r/m,(r+1)/m]$, whose side ratio is $(M/n)/(1/m)=M'$, and conformal maps preserve the modulus. The inverse chart is $F=\Phi^{-1}\circ L$ with $L(\xi,\eta)=\bigl(M(\xi+k)/n,\ (\eta+r)/m\bigr)$; since $\Phi^{-1}$ is conformal, $\mu_F=\mu_L=(a-b)/(a+b)$ with $a=M/n$ and $b=1/m$, which is $(M'-1)/(M'+1)$; the formula for $J\,G^{-1}$ follows from \eqref{eq:metric-mu}. Level curves of $u$ meet the walls at right angles because $\partial_nu=0$ there, and level curves of $u$ and $v$ meet each other at right angles because $\Phi$ is conformal. ∎

Choosing $n=\operatorname{round}(Mm)$ makes every piece's modulus close to one, so the conformal chart of every interior piece has $\mathsf A\approx\hat\kappa I$. The user, or the layout, chooses the number $m$ of pieces across; the number along follows. On the s-channel of Figure 2, $M=8.872$; nine pieces along give a predicted modulus of $0.986$, and the modulus computed independently on each piece lies between $0.977$ and $0.988$ measured. With $18\times2$ pieces the computed moduli lie between $0.976$ and $1.051$. On a second test geometry, a bend with three arms (bend-3, $M=1.731$), a $5\times3$ cut predicts $1.039$ and gives $1.038$ to $1.067$. Equal modulus is not equal area: the nine s-channel pieces contain between 398 and 698 grid cells. Every piece nevertheless costs one network evaluation on the same reference grid.

4.2Geometry: certified charts

What the network receives. By \eqref{eq:pullback} and \eqref{eq:metric-mu}, the pulled-back problem on a piece with scalar conductivity is determined by $\hat\kappa$, $\mu$ and $J$. The network therefore receives, on an $n\times n$ reference grid,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:inputs} \bigl(\log J-\overline{\log J},\ \operatorname{Re}\mu,\ \operatorname{Im}\mu,\ \log\hat\kappa,\ \hat f\bigr)\quad\text{and the scalars}\quad(\Fo,\ \text{physical parameters}), \end{equation}

where $\overline{\log J}$ is the mean of $\log J$ over the square, $\hat f$ is the pulled-back source, and the Fourier number \eqref{eq:fourier} is formed with $L^2$ the area of the piece, so that the size of the piece enters only through $\Fo$. Translating, rotating or uniformly scaling the piece leaves every input field unchanged; for instance, a rotation $F\mapsto e^{i\varphi}F$ multiplies $F_z$ and $F_{\bar z}$ by the same factor and leaves $\mu$ unchanged. The network therefore never has to learn these invariances. The Beltrami coefficient is bounded by one in modulus, which makes it a better-conditioned input than the entries of $\mathsf A$, which are unbounded as $|\mu|\to1$. For tensor conductivities and vector-valued problems, the inputs are extended by the remaining entries of $\mathsf A$ and by the rotation angle of $DF$; this paper treats scalar conductivity.

The corner trade-off. Interior pieces have right-angled corners (Proposition 1), but where a cut or an end edge meets a wall of the physical domain the corner can have any angle. A chart must then distort the square somewhere.

Proposition 2 (Distortion at a corner.)

Let $F$ be a chart that is continuously differentiable up to a corner of the square, with $DF$ nonsingular there, and let the two sides of the piece that meet at the image of that corner form the interior angle $\alpha$. Then $\alpha\lt\pi$, and the dilatation of $F$ at the corner satisfies

\begin{equation}\label{eq:corner} K\ \ge\ \max\Bigl(\tan\frac\alpha2,\ \cot\frac\alpha2\Bigr). \end{equation}

The proof, which reduces to the singular values of a linear map that sends a right angle to the angle $\alpha$, is in Appendix B. A conformal chart has $K=1$ and escapes the bound only by being singular at the corner: near a corner of angle $\alpha$ its Jacobian behaves like $J\sim r^{\,2-\pi/\alpha}$, with $r$ the distance to the corner, so $J$ is unbounded at an acute corner and vanishes at an obtuse one. The end piece of the s-channel has corners of $68^\circ$ and $148^\circ$, for which \eqref{eq:corner} requires $K\ge1.48$ and $K\ge3.49$ respectively (the latter means $|\mu|\ge0.55$). Table 2 and Figure 3 compare three charts of that piece. The conformal chart keeps $\mathsf A$ within 7% of isotropic but lets $J$ vary by a factor of 291; the Winslow chart and transfinite interpolation keep $J$ within a factor of 3.2 and 4.1 at the price of an off-diagonal coefficient $|\mathsf A_{12}|$ of $0.81$ and $0.95$ at the 95th percentile. The transfinite chart, which is smooth up to the corners, reaches a dilatation of $4.53$, consistent with the bound.

Re-entrant corners. At a corner with $\alpha\gt\pi$ no chart that is smooth up to the corner exists, and the solution of \eqref{eq:model} is itself singular: near the corner it behaves like $r^{\pi/\alpha}$, whose gradient is unbounded. The conformal chart removes this singularity from the solution. With $w$ a complex coordinate centred at the corner, the harmonic function $\operatorname{Im}w^{\pi/\alpha}$ is pulled back by the local chart $w=\zeta^{2\alpha/\pi}$ to $\operatorname{Im}\zeta^2=2\xi\eta$, a polynomial. The singularity is moved into $J$, which is integrable, and the network is asked to represent a smooth function.

The chart rule. Table 3 states which chart is used. The choice between the conformal and the Winslow chart is made by the envelope defined below, not by a threshold on the angle: when the conformal chart's range of $\log J$ exceeds the declared bound, the Winslow chart is used.

The certificate. In practice a chart is represented by the images $X_{ij}$ of the nodes of the $(n+1)\times(n+1)$ reference grid and interpolated bilinearly on each cell. Its injectivity can be verified exactly.

Proposition 3 (Discrete chart certificate.)

Let $F$ be the bilinear interpolant of nodal values $X_{ij}$ on the reference grid. Suppose that (a) the Jacobian determinant of $F$ is positive at the four corners of every cell, equivalently every image cell is a strictly convex quadrilateral with positive orientation; and (b) the boundary nodes, taken in order, form a simple closed polygon with positive orientation. Then $F$ is a homeomorphism of the closed square onto the closed region bounded by that polygon.

Proof. On a cell, $F(\xi,\eta)=\mathbf p+\mathbf b\xi+\mathbf c\eta+\mathbf d\xi\eta$ in local coordinates, so $\det DF=\mathbf b\times\mathbf c+\xi\,(\mathbf b\times\mathbf d)+\eta\,(\mathbf d\times\mathbf c)$, the term in $\xi\eta$ vanishing because $\mathbf d\times\mathbf d=0$. The determinant is affine on the cell, hence positive on the whole cell if it is positive at its corners, and $F$ is a local diffeomorphism inside each cell. Adjacent cells share an edge on which both interpolants are the same linear function, and by positive orientation their images lie on opposite sides of it. At an interior node, the four image cells have angles in $(0,\pi)$ that close up around the node, so their sum is a positive multiple of $2\pi$ that is less than $4\pi$, hence equal to $2\pi$; the images do not overlap near the node. Thus $F$ is a local homeomorphism on the open square. For a point $y$ not on the boundary polygon, the number of preimages of $y$ equals the winding number of the polygon about $y$, by the argument principle for local homeomorphisms (the degree of $F$ at $y$ is computed from the boundary). By (b) this number is $1$ inside the polygon and $0$ outside, so $F$ is a bijection onto the closed region and, as a continuous bijection of a compact space onto a Hausdorff space, a homeomorphism. ∎

The test costs $O(n^2)$ operations and is exact in rational arithmetic. Discrete harmonic maps of triangulations have an analogous guarantee by construction [30]; for curved finite elements in three dimensions, the corresponding test bounds the Jacobian through its Bézier coefficients [31]. The certificate applies to any chart, including one produced by a network. Learned charts [32] are nevertheless not used here, because the pull-back \eqref{eq:pullback} is exact for every valid chart: nothing about the map needs to be learned, and a learned map would change the network's inputs whenever it was retrained.

The envelope. Each trained network declares the range of inputs on which it is trusted:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:envelope} \sup|\mu|\le k_\mu,\qquad \max\log J-\min\log J\le\ell_J,\qquad \lVert\hat\nabla\log J\rVert_\infty+\lVert\hat\nabla\mu\rVert_\infty\le s, \end{equation}

together with ranges for $\hat\kappa$ and $\Fo$. The smoothness bound is included because the error of neural operators has been found to follow the frequency content of their input more closely than its range or its topology [33]. The host measures each chart and refuses one outside the envelope, naming the violated quantity; the layout can then cut the piece further. On bend-3, the conformal $J_{\max}/J_{\min}$ of the worst piece falls from 10 with $2\times1$ pieces to 2.7 with $5\times3$ measured.

The geometry source. The project's workbench stores a domain as a mask of grid cells, whose boundary is a staircase. A chart of the staircase would present the network with spurious corners on every step. Charts are therefore computed from the smooth outline as drawn, and the classical reference solution is computed in chart coordinates on the same reference grid by the same finite-volume scheme. This gives a reference solution of the network's own class at any resolution (row S8) and a classical subdomain solver that is exactly comparable with the network, which gate E3 of Section 8 requires.

4.3The expert's output: constraint modes

One question for every network. Every network answers the same question: given values on all four sides of the unit square, what is the field inside, and what flux leaves through each side? The all-Dirichlet problem contains all of a piece's linear boundary behaviour. Whether a side is a wall with prescribed temperature, an insulated wall, a Robin condition or an interface with a neighbour is decided by the host, which imposes it as a linear relation between the side's port coordinates and its flux: a prescribed flux becomes a load on that side's coordinates, and a Robin condition adds a symmetric positive semidefinite term to its port matrix. No network can acquire its own conventions for boundary conditions.

The discrete energy of a piece. On the reference grid with $n\times n$ cells, the unknowns of a piece are the cell values $u\in\R^{n^2}$ and the values $\lambda\in\R^{4n}$ on its boundary faces. The discrete energy of the pulled-back problem is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:piece-energy} \mathcal E(u,\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}u\\ \lambda\end{pmatrix}^{\!\top}\!M\begin{pmatrix}u\\ \lambda\end{pmatrix},\qquad M=\begin{pmatrix}A_I & -B\\ -B^\top & D\end{pmatrix}, \end{equation}

a sum of squares: the interior face conductances times squared differences of neighbouring cell values, the half-cell conductances of boundary faces times squared differences between a cell value and its face value, and the capacity term $J\hat\sigma$ times squared cell values. $M$ is symmetric positive semidefinite, and positive definite when $\sigma\gt0$. $A_I$ is positive definite, because a boundary face holds every cell to its face value.

Constraint modes and the particular field. With the port basis $Q\in\R^{4n\times4m}$ of Section 2.4, the exact constraint modes, the exact particular field and the exact port matrix of the piece are

\begin{equation}\label{eq:modes} \begin{gathered}H=A_I^{-1}BQ\in\R^{n^2\times4m},\qquad u_p=A_I^{-1}f,\qquad V=\begin{pmatrix}H\\ Q\end{pmatrix},\\ \Lambda=V^\top MV=Q^\top\bigl(D-B^\top A_I^{-1}B\bigr)Q.\end{gathered} \end{equation}

By the discrete Dirichlet principle, $H_k$, the $k$-th column of $H$, is the interior field of least energy among those paired with the boundary values $Qe_k$; $u_p$ is the response to the source with zero boundary values.

What the network returns. The network returns approximations of $H$ and $u_p$ in the form

\begin{equation}\label{eq:learned} \hat H_k=H_k^{(0)}+\beta\odot N_{\theta,k}(z),\qquad \hat u_p=\beta\odot N_{\theta,p}(z),\qquad \beta(\xi,\eta)=16\,\xi(1-\xi)\,\eta(1-\eta), \end{equation}

where $z$ denotes the inputs \eqref{eq:inputs}, $N_{\theta,k}$ and $N_{\theta,p}$ are output channels of one network, $\odot$ is the entrywise product on the grid, and $H^{(0)}_k$ is the principal part: the exact constraint mode \eqref{eq:modes} of the plain square, with $\mathsf A=I$ and the capacity of the given Fourier number. The principal part is computed once for each resolution and Fourier number, by a fast sine transform in $O(n^2\log n)$ operations, and stored. Its continuum counterpart for the bottom side is given in Appendix B; Figure 4 shows three of them. One forward pass returns all $4m+1$ fields, 65 for $m=16$.

Why a principal part and a bubble. The correction $H_k-H_k^{(0)}$ is the effect of the departure of $\mathsf A$ and $J\hat\sigma$ from those of the plain square. Equal-value cuts make that departure small on interior pieces, so the network represents a small field rather than the whole response. That this makes learning easier is a hypothesis, tested by the input-class comparison of Section 8. In the face-based discretisation the boundary values form the separate vector $\lambda=Qe_k$, so any interior field paired with it has exactly the prescribed boundary values; the network cannot violate them. The bubble $\beta$, which vanishes on the boundary and equals one at the centre, additionally makes the correction vanish towards the boundary, as $H_k-H_k^{(0)}$ does, since both fields share their boundary values.

Remark 4 (Port modes at corners.)

The cosine modes of two adjacent sides are independent, so the boundary values of a single mode jump at a corner of the square. In the continuum such boundary values have infinite energy; in the face-based discretisation the corner carries no unknown and the energy is finite, but it grows logarithmically as the grid is refined. At the resolutions used here the effect is contained in the measured truncation error (Table 8). A conforming basis, with sine modes that vanish at the corners and four corner functions, is registered as a comparison.

Symmetry. The symmetry group of the square, generated by the rotation through $90^\circ$ and a reflection, acts on the inputs and permutes the port modes; the exact solution operator commutes with this action. Each group element acts on the Beltrami coefficient by a fixed rule; for example, rotating the reference coordinates by $90^\circ$ about the centre of the square replaces $\mu$ by $-\mu$. At Stage 0, the first training stage (Section 6), the symmetry is imposed by training on all eight transformed copies of each example; architectures that are equivariant by construction are later options.

4.4The backbone

The network maps the five input fields and the scalars of \eqref{eq:inputs} on the $n\times n$ grid to $4m+1$ output fields. Because the constraint modes solve an elliptic problem, every output value depends on every input value, so the network needs a receptive field covering the whole square. Because the output format of Section 4.3 guarantees the properties the coupling needs (Section 5.1), the network itself needs no certified property, such as a bound on its Lipschitz constant, when the host couples the pieces in this way (tier 0 of Section 5.2), which leaves the choice of architecture free.

The default is a U-shaped convolutional operator in the manner of the convolutional neural operator [22]: four resolution levels, two $3\times3$ convolutions per level with GELU activations, averaging to coarsen and bilinear interpolation to refine, skip connections, and FiLM conditioning on the scalars. The convolutional neural operator's band-limiting filters act on the periodic extension of the signal, which is inappropriate on a square whose opposite sides are unrelated; we use explicit extensions instead, even for the inputs and odd for the outputs, whose corrections vanish on the boundary. The registered alternative is a spectral operator in the sine and cosine bases of the square, whose sine series vanish on the boundary exactly as the corrections must. The default size is about $10^6$ parameters, between the measured small and medium configurations of Table 9, and a sweep over $\{0.3,1,3\}\times10^6$ parameters is part of the Stage 0 gate. The reference grid has $n=64$, and training mixes $n=32$, $64$ and $128$. Training is in single precision; evaluation can run in double precision, so that finite-difference probes of the network are not limited by rounding (row S10).

4.5Ports, conservation and time

Interface data. At tier 0 the interface data are the port coordinates, 16 cosine coefficients per side. Two pieces that share a side parametrise it differently in general, because each chart distributes the side's arc length over $[0,1]$ in its own way. The host transfers port coordinates between the two parametrisations by an $L^2$ projection with respect to physical arc length, the construction known as a mortar coupling. Each network carries about 20 modes per side internally, so that the 16 modes exchanged with the neighbour are represented after this reparametrisation with small loss; the number 20 is an estimate to be checked at Stage 0. Tier-2 coupling (Section 5.3) exchanges Robin or wave coordinates instead, which the host computes from the same output.

Conservation. For equations in conservation form, such as advection–diffusion, a second output format is defined: the network returns fluxes $\hat{\mathbf F}$ on the faces of the reference grid, and the update is their exact discrete divergence,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:conservative} J\,\frac{\hat u^{\,\text{new}}-\hat u^{\,\text{old}}}{\Delta t}=-\hat\nabla_h\cdot\hat{\mathbf F}+J\hat s, \end{equation}

so that the conserved quantity is balanced exactly in every cell and the flux a side sends to its neighbour is the flux the piece used in its own balance. This format is required for conservation-law families from Stage 1 and optional for diffusion.

Time. The time step enters as the Fourier number of the piece, a scalar input. Each network declares the range of $\Fo$ on which it was trained, and the host refuses a call outside that range, which makes row S2's failure a declared refusal rather than a silent error. The networks of tier 0 approximate the implicit Euler step at the given Fourier number, which is the quantity the coupled solver uses; their training covers the declared range of $\Fo$ with exact examples (Section 6). At a fixed step and fixed geometry the constraint modes do not change, so they are computed once; each later step needs one forward pass for the new particular field and one back-substitution in the factorised interface system.

5Coupling and its guarantee

This section states how the host couples the pieces and what can be proved about the result. Section 5.1 treats linear problems, where the coupling is a single linear solve and the guarantee holds for every network output. Section 5.2 places this in a three-tier scheme that also covers nonlinear problems and networks that can only be used as black boxes, and Section 5.3 gives the guarantee available for the latter, which is conditional on a checkable property of each network.

5.1The energy Gram

Assembly. Let the pieces be $i=1,\dots,P$, with energy matrices $M_i$ as in \eqref{eq:piece-energy}, port bases $Q_i$, sources $f_i$, and network outputs $\hat H_i$ and $\hat u_{p,i}$ as in \eqref{eq:learned}. The global unknown is a vector $c$ of port coordinates with one block of $m$ coefficients per side; a side shared by two pieces carries one block, used by both. The matrix $R_i$ selects the $4m$ coordinates of piece $i$, with the orientation of each side as seen from that piece. Coordinates on sides with prescribed boundary values are fixed. With $\tilde V_i=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\hat H_i\\ Q_i\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$, the host computes

\begin{equation}\label{eq:gram} \tilde\Lambda_i=\tilde V_i^\top M_i\tilde V_i,\qquad \tilde b_i=\hat H_i^\top f_i-\tilde V_i^\top M_i\begin{pmatrix}\hat u_{p,i}\\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \end{equation}

assembles and solves, for the free coordinates,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:assembled} \tilde{\mathsf S}\,c=\tilde\chi,\qquad \tilde{\mathsf S}=\sum_{i=1}^PR_i^\top\tilde\Lambda_iR_i,\qquad \tilde\chi=\sum_{i=1}^PR_i^\top\tilde b_i, \end{equation}

with the contribution of the fixed coordinates moved to the right-hand side, and reconstructs each piece as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:reconstruct} \tilde u_i=\hat u_{p,i}+\hat H_iR_ic,\qquad \lambda_i=Q_iR_ic. \end{equation}

The reconstruction is a linear combination of fields the network has already returned, so a linear steady solve costs one forward pass per piece. Replacing $\hat H_i$ and $\hat u_{p,i}$ by the exact $H_i$ and $u_{p,i}$ of \eqref{eq:modes} gives the classical port-reduced superelement, whose solution we denote by $u_m$ and whose interface matrix by $\mathsf S$.

The setting of the theorems. Let $U$ be the set of global discrete fields: cell values on every piece and face values on every boundary face, such that the two pieces sharing a face assign it the same value and the faces on the physical boundary carry the prescribed values. Let $U_0$ be the corresponding linear space with zero prescribed values, and define, for $w\in U$,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:global-energy} \mathcal E(w)=\sum_{i=1}^P\Bigl(\tfrac12\,w_i^\top M_iw_i-f_i^\top w_{I,i}\Bigr),\qquad \lVert w\rVert_M^2=\sum_{i=1}^Pw_i^\top M_iw_i, \end{equation}

where $w_i$ is the restriction of $w$ to piece $i$ and $w_{I,i}$ its cell values. Eliminating a shared face value between two neighbouring cells reproduces the harmonic-mean face conductance, so the minimiser $u$ of $\mathcal E$ over $U$ is the ordinary cell-centred finite-volume solution of the whole problem. We assume that the problem is well posed, so that $\lVert\cdot\rVert_M$ is a norm on $U_0$; that shared sides are parametrised identically by the two charts, so that $R_i$ is a selection with orientation; and that the prescribed boundary values lie in the span of the port modes. The last two assumptions are discussed after the proofs. Under them, the trial set

\begin{equation}\label{eq:trialset} \tilde W=\Bigl\{w\in U:\ w_i=\begin{pmatrix}\hat u_{p,i}\\ 0\end{pmatrix}+\tilde V_iR_ic\ \text{ for some }c\Bigr\} \end{equation}

is contained in $U$. For symmetric matrices, $X\succeq Y$ means that $X-Y$ is positive semidefinite.

Theorem 5 (The Gram port matrix.)

Let $\hat H_i\in\R^{n^2\times4m}$ be arbitrary, and write $E_i=\hat H_i-H_i$ for the error of the network's constraint modes. Then:

  1. $\tilde\Lambda_i$ is symmetric positive semidefinite, and positive definite whenever $M_i$ is.
  2. $\tilde\Lambda_i-\Lambda_i=E_i^\top A_{I,i}E_i$. Consequently $\tilde\Lambda_i\succeq\Lambda_i$ in the order of symmetric matrices, $\lVert\tilde\Lambda_i-\Lambda_i\rVert_2\le\lVert A_{I,i}\rVert_2\,\lVert E_i\rVert_2^2$, and $\tr\tilde\Lambda_i-\tr\Lambda_i=\sum_k\lVert E_{i,k}\rVert_{A_{I,i}}^2$.
  3. $\tilde{\mathsf S}\succeq\mathsf S$. If $\mathsf S$ is positive definite, then so is $\tilde{\mathsf S}$, with $\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{\mathsf S})\ge\lambda_{\min}(\mathsf S)$, and \eqref{eq:assembled} has a unique solution for every network output.
Proof. (i) For every $x$, $x^\top\tilde\Lambda_ix=(\tilde V_ix)^\top M_i(\tilde V_ix)\ge0$, since $M_i$ is positive semidefinite. If $M_i$ is positive definite, $\tilde V_ix=0$ forces $Q_ix=0$, hence $x=0$, because $Q_i$ has orthonormal columns. (ii) Write $\tilde V_i=V_i+\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}E_i\\ 0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ with $V_i$ the exact matrix of \eqref{eq:modes}. Then
$$\tilde\Lambda_i=V_i^\top M_iV_i+\begin{pmatrix}E_i\\ 0\end{pmatrix}^{\!\top}\!M_iV_i+V_i^\top M_i\begin{pmatrix}E_i\\ 0\end{pmatrix}+\begin{pmatrix}E_i\\ 0\end{pmatrix}^{\!\top}\!M_i\begin{pmatrix}E_i\\ 0\end{pmatrix}.$$
The interior rows of $M_iV_i$ are $A_{I,i}H_i-B_iQ_i=0$ by the definition of $H_i$, so both cross terms vanish, and the last term equals $E_i^\top A_{I,i}E_i$. This matrix is positive semidefinite; its norm is at most $\lVert A_{I,i}\rVert_2\lVert E_i\rVert_2^2$; and its $k$-th diagonal entry is $\lVert E_{i,k}\rVert^2_{A_{I,i}}$. (iii) $\tilde{\mathsf S}-\mathsf S=\sum_iR_i^\top(\tilde\Lambda_i-\Lambda_i)R_i$ is a sum of positive semidefinite matrices by (ii). The smallest eigenvalue is monotone in the order of symmetric matrices, by the Rayleigh quotient. ∎

In words: an error in the network's fields can make a piece stiffer in the energy sense, never softer, and the excess stiffness is exactly the energy of the field errors. No output can make a piece singular or indefinite, which is the kind of failure recorded in row S6, and which a network predicting the port matrix directly does exhibit (below). The error of the port matrix is quadratic in the error of the fields: a 1% error in the fields gives an error of order $10^{-4}$ in the port matrix.

Theorem 6 (Energy optimality.)

Under the assumptions above, the reconstruction $\tilde u$ of \eqref{eq:reconstruct} satisfies

\begin{equation}\label{eq:cea} \lVert u-\tilde u\rVert_M=\min_{w\in\tilde W}\lVert u-w\rVert_M . \end{equation}

Moreover, if $c^\star$ denotes the port coordinates of the classical superelement solution $u_m$ and $e_{p,i}=\hat u_{p,i}-u_{p,i}$, then

\begin{equation}\label{eq:cea-bound} \lVert u-\tilde u\rVert_M\ \le\ \lVert u-u_m\rVert_M+\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^P\bigl\lVert e_{p,i}+E_iR_ic^\star\bigr\rVert_{A_{I,i}}^2\Bigr)^{1/2}. \end{equation}
Proof. For $w\in U$ the difference $w-u$ lies in $U_0$, and the first variation of $\mathcal E$ at its minimiser $u$ vanishes in every direction of $U_0$; since $\mathcal E$ is quadratic, $\mathcal E(w)=\mathcal E(u)+\tfrac12\lVert w-u\rVert_M^2$. On $\tilde W$, parametrised by $c$, the gradient of $\mathcal E$ is $\sum_iR_i^\top\bigl(\tilde V_i^\top M_i\tilde V_iR_ic+\tilde V_i^\top M_i(\hat u_{p,i},0)^\top-\hat H_i^\top f_i\bigr)=\tilde{\mathsf S}c-\tilde\chi$, so \eqref{eq:assembled} states that $\tilde u$ minimises $\mathcal E$, hence $\lVert u-\cdot\rVert_M$, over $\tilde W$. For the bound, let $w^\star\in\tilde W$ have coordinates $c^\star$. Then $w^\star-u_m$ has cell values $e_{p,i}+E_iR_ic^\star$ on piece $i$ and zero face values, and for such a field $\lVert(e,0)\rVert^2_{M_i}=e^\top A_{I,i}e$. The triangle inequality and \eqref{eq:cea} give \eqref{eq:cea-bound}. ∎

The identity \eqref{eq:cea} is Céa's lemma [34] with constant one, which holds because the problem is symmetric: the coupled solution is the best approximation of the exact one, in the energy norm, among all fields the network's modes can represent. The coupling cannot amplify the network's error; \eqref{eq:cea-bound} separates the error into the truncation error of the classical superelement and the network's own field errors, weighted by the actual port coordinates.

On the assumptions. When the two charts of a shared side parametrise it differently, the mortar projection of Section 4.5 makes the face values of the two pieces agree only in the projected sense, $\tilde W$ is no longer contained in $U$, and the method becomes a nonconforming one; Theorem 5 still holds piece by piece, but \eqref{eq:cea} acquires a consistency term that we have not analysed. Prescribed boundary values that are not in the span of the port modes are replaced by their projection, whose effect adds to the truncation error $\lVert u-u_m\rVert_M$.

Measurement. The probe scripts/arch_superelement_gram.py compares the Gram construction with a network head that would output the port matrix directly. It uses the reference-space problem of Section 7: $4\times4$ square pieces of $32\times32$ cells, a smooth log-normal conductivity, zero boundary values on the outer boundary and a smooth source, with 16 cosine modes per side (384 interface unknowns). The exact constraint modes and port matrices are computed classically and then perturbed by the same relative amount $\varepsilon$, standing in for a network of that accuracy: the modes by smooth random fields scaled to a relative energy error $\varepsilon$ per mode, the port matrices by symmetric random matrices of relative Frobenius norm $\varepsilon$. The direct head is given its best case, exact particular fields and loads. Table 4 and Figure 5 show the outcome. The Gram port matrices have errors close to $0.9\,\varepsilon^2$ and stay positive semidefinite; the identity of Theorem 5(ii) holds to $2\times10^{-15}$. The direct head's port matrices are indefinite for three or more of the sixteen pieces at every $\varepsilon$ in the steady case: the exact port matrix of a steady piece is singular, since a uniform temperature carries no flux, and an arbitrarily small symmetric perturbation can make it indefinite. At $\varepsilon\ge0.1$ the direct head's assembled interface matrix is itself indefinite and its solution error exceeds 100%. At $\varepsilon=0.01$ the coupled solution error is 0.27% with the Gram and 9.2% with the direct head (steady), and 0.12% against 1.9% at $\Fo=0.1$ measured.

Proposition 7 (Perturbation of the interface solve.)

Let $\mathsf S$ and $\tilde{\mathsf S}$ be symmetric positive definite, $\mathsf Sc=\chi$ and $\tilde{\mathsf S}\tilde c=\tilde\chi$. Then

\begin{equation}\label{eq:perturb} \lVert\tilde c-c\rVert_2\le\frac{\lVert\mathsf S-\tilde{\mathsf S}\rVert_2\,\lVert c\rVert_2+\lVert\chi-\tilde\chi\rVert_2}{\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{\mathsf S})}. \end{equation}

For the Gram construction, $\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{\mathsf S})\ge\lambda_{\min}(\mathsf S)$ and $\lVert\mathsf S-\tilde{\mathsf S}\rVert_2\le\sum_i\lVert A_{I,i}\rVert_2\lVert E_i\rVert_2^2$, by Theorem 5.

Proof. $\tilde{\mathsf S}(\tilde c-c)=(\tilde\chi-\chi)+(\mathsf S-\tilde{\mathsf S})c$; multiply by $\tilde{\mathsf S}^{-1}$, whose norm is $1/\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{\mathsf S})$, and use $\lVert R_i\rVert_2\le1$ for the selection matrices. ∎

Status of the proofs. Theorem 5, Theorem 6 and Proposition 7 are statements of finite-dimensional linear algebra, and their proofs are complete as given. They have not been machine-checked. Their formalisation in the Lean 4 proof assistant, together with the convergence results of Section 5.3, is part of the project's proof plan (Section 11). The numerical check of the identity in Theorem 5(ii) is a test of the implementation, not a proof.

5.2Three tiers of coupling

The host chooses a tier for each interface according to what the networks on either side provide (Table 5).

A tier-0 network also serves at tier 2: from its port matrix $\tilde\Lambda$ the host forms the scattering map of \eqref{eq:cayley} below for any impedance, and by Theorem 5(i) and Lemma 8 that map is non-expansive. At tier 2 the accelerator is GMRES when the network provides Jacobian–vector products and Anderson acceleration otherwise, and a coarse space is used from 16 pieces on, where the measured round counts of one-level methods begin to grow (Figure 6). Sweeps that solve the pieces of a chain in order, which can carry information along a channel in a single pass, are permitted at tier 2 only, and not where the project averages a solver's output over the symmetry group of the problem, because that averaging is exact only for updates that do not depend on the order of the pieces.

Time-dependent problems. At a fixed step and fixed geometry the port matrices are formed once and reused, so each later step costs one forward pass per piece and one back-substitution. Each step starts from the interface values of the previous step, extrapolated in time. Where the coupling is formulated so that it is energy-stable after any finite number of exchanges, as in the wave-domain splitting of [35], a tier-2 step may stop early; the unconverged remainder is then an accuracy term in the error bound, not a stability risk. Small steps help every scheme: at $\Fo=0.01$ the measured round counts of all iterative schemes fall to between 13 and 40, because an implicit step only couples pieces within a diffusion length.

5.3Iterative coupling with certified contraction

Wave variables. On a side of a piece, let $e$ be the vector of boundary values (the temperature) and $f=\Lambda e$ the heat flux into the piece. For an impedance $Z\gt0$ define the incoming and outgoing waves

\begin{equation}\label{eq:waves} a=\frac{e+Zf}{2\sqrt Z},\qquad b=\frac{e-Zf}{2\sqrt Z},\qquad\text{so that}\qquad\langle e,f\rangle=\lVert a\rVert^2-\lVert b\rVert^2 . \end{equation}

The power entering the piece is the incoming wave power minus the outgoing. On a shared side the temperatures agree and the fluxes are opposite, $f_i=-f_j$, so at the solution $a_i=b_j$: the neighbour's outgoing wave is the piece's incoming wave. Imposing $a_i=b_j$ is exactly the Robin condition \eqref{eq:robin} with $p=1/Z$. A source-free piece maps incoming to outgoing waves by its scattering map

\begin{equation}\label{eq:cayley} \mathcal S=(I-Z\Lambda)(I+Z\Lambda)^{-1}, \end{equation}

the Cayley transform of its Dirichlet-to-Neumann map.

Lemma 8 (Passive pieces do not amplify waves.)

If $\Lambda$ is symmetric positive semidefinite and $Z\gt0$, then $\mathcal S$ exists and $\lVert\mathcal S\rVert_2\le1$. If moreover $\langle\Lambda e,e\rangle\ge\gamma\lVert e\rVert^2$ and $\lVert\Lambda\rVert_2\le\Gamma$, then $\lVert\mathcal S\rVert_2^2\le1-4Z\gamma/(1+Z\Gamma)^2$.

Proof. $I+Z\Lambda$ has eigenvalues at least one, so it is invertible. For $e\ne0$ put $y=(I+Z\Lambda)e$. Expanding, $\lVert(I-Z\Lambda)e\rVert^2=\lVert y\rVert^2-4Z\langle\Lambda e,e\rangle\le\lVert y\rVert^2$, which is the first claim. Under the further hypotheses, $4Z\langle\Lambda e,e\rangle\ge4Z\gamma\lVert e\rVert^2\ge4Z\gamma\lVert y\rVert^2/(1+Z\Gamma)^2$, since $\lVert y\rVert\le(1+Z\Gamma)\lVert e\rVert$. ∎

The global iteration is $a^{r+1}=\Pi\,\tilde{\mathcal S}(a^r)+g$, where $\tilde{\mathcal S}$ applies each piece's (learned) scattering map, $\Pi$ hands each side's outgoing wave to the neighbour, an isometry, and $g$ collects sources and boundary data. For exact pieces the lemma makes each round non-expansive. For a learned black box nothing of the kind is automatic, and the following classical result is the guarantee available.

Theorem 9 (Perturbed contraction.)

Let $(X,d)$ be a complete metric space, let $T:X\to X$ have a fixed point $a^\star$, and let $\tilde T:X\to X$ satisfy $d(\tilde Ta,\tilde Ta')\le\rho\,d(a,a')$ with $\rho\lt1$ and $d(\tilde Ta,Ta)\le\delta$ for all $a,a'$. Then $\tilde T$ has a unique fixed point $\tilde a^\star$, the iterates satisfy $d(\tilde T^ra_0,\tilde a^\star)\le\rho^r\,d(a_0,\tilde a^\star)$ from every $a_0$, and

\begin{equation}\label{eq:perturbed-contraction} d(\tilde a^\star,a^\star)\le\frac{\delta}{1-\rho}. \end{equation}
Proof. Existence, uniqueness and the rate are Banach's fixed-point theorem. Then
$$d(\tilde a^\star,a^\star)=d(\tilde T\tilde a^\star,Ta^\star)\le d(\tilde T\tilde a^\star,\tilde Ta^\star)+d(\tilde Ta^\star,Ta^\star)\le\rho\,d(\tilde a^\star,a^\star)+\delta,$$
and the bound follows by rearranging. ∎

Applied with $T$ the classical wave iteration and $\tilde T$ the learned one, the theorem makes convergence a property of the network that can be checked, its contraction factor $\rho$, rather than a consequence of how well it was trained; accuracy enters only through the distance $\delta/(1-\rho)$ between the learned and the classical limits. With a coarse space, the slowly converging components are solved directly, and the contraction hypothesis is needed only on the complement of the coarse space, which we call the fine space. The hypothesis cannot be weakened to accuracy alone:

Remark 10 (A counterexample.)

Theorem 1 of [36] assumes that (a) the classical additive Schwarz–Richardson map contracts with factor $\rho\lt1$ and (b) the learned subdomain solver differs from the exact one by at most $c$, uniformly, and concludes that the learned iteration converges, to a limit within a distance proportional to $c/(1-\rho)$ of the classical one. The distance bound is correct; the convergence step does not follow. Here $\tau$ denotes the relaxation parameter of the iteration. Take one subdomain, an exact solver $S(u)=0$, so that the classical map $F(u)=(1-\tau)u$ contracts with $\rho=1-\tau$, and the learned solver $\tilde S(u)=-c\tanh(ku)$, which satisfies $|\tilde S-S|\le c$. The learned map $\tilde F(u)=(1-\tau)u+\tau\tilde S(u)$ has the single fixed point $0$, with $\tilde F'(0)=1-\tau-\tau ck=-4.5$ for $\tau=\tfrac12$, $c=1$, $k=10$, so the fixed point repels; since $|\tilde F(u)|\le|u|/2+1/2$, every orbit stays bounded, and none starting away from $0$ converges. From $u_0=1$ the iterates settle on the two-cycle $\pm0.332471$ (scripts/arch_sni_counterexample.py). Both hypotheses hold and the conclusion fails; the hypothesis $\Lip(\tilde F)\lt1$ of Theorem 9 restores it.

Establishing the contraction factor. A bound on the Lipschitz constant of a trained network can be certified by propagating intervals or linear relaxations through its layers [37]; such bound propagation has been formalised in Lean 4 [38]. These bounds can be loose for networks with millions of parameters. The fallback is architectural: a classical scattering map of known contraction factor plus a small learned correction whose bound is certified. Tier 0 requires none of this, which is the main reason to prefer it wherever the physics is linear.

Status. Lemma 8 and Theorem 9 are classical, and their proofs are given above for completeness; the two-level statement for learned maps is stated in the project's proof plan and is not proved here. None of these results has been machine-checked.

6Training

Data. Training examples are generated by the project's own classical solver, which makes them unlimited in number, free of licence restrictions, and matched exactly to the output format. An example is a random piece with its chart, coefficient and source. Pieces are random closed spline curves cut into four sides and charted by the rule of Table 3; they are binned by $\sup|\mu|$ and by the range of $\log J$, so that coverage of the envelope \eqref{eq:envelope} can be measured. Log-conductivities and sources are smooth Gaussian random fields, with material interfaces along random spline curves, and the Fourier number is drawn log-uniformly from the declared range. The labels, the exact constraint modes and particular field \eqref{eq:modes}, cost one sparse factorisation of $A_I$ and $4m+1$ pairs of triangular solves per example.

Objective. Errors are measured in the energy norm of the example's own problem, which by Theorem 5(ii) is the norm that controls the error of the port matrix:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss} \mathcal L_{\text{sup}}(\theta)=\mathbb E\Bigl[\ \sum_{k=1}^{4m}\lVert\hat H_k(\theta)-H_k\rVert_{A_I}^2+\lVert\hat u_p(\theta)-u_p\rVert_{A_I}^2\Bigr], \end{equation}

with the expectation over examples and the eight symmetries of the square. The theorem of Section 5.1 yields a second objective that needs no labels.

Corollary 11 (A label-free objective.)

For one example, define $\mathcal L_{\text{en}}(\theta)=\tr\tilde\Lambda(\theta)+\hat u_p(\theta)^\top A_I\,\hat u_p(\theta)-2f^\top\hat u_p(\theta)$, which requires only the network's output and the matrix $M$ of \eqref{eq:piece-energy}. Then $\mathcal L_{\text{en}}(\theta)$ and the summand of \eqref{eq:loss} differ by a constant that does not depend on $\theta$. In particular they have the same gradients with respect to $\theta$ and the same minimisers.

Proof. By Theorem 5(ii), $\tr\tilde\Lambda=\tr\Lambda+\sum_k\lVert\hat H_k-H_k\rVert_{A_I}^2$. Since $u_p=A_I^{-1}f$ minimises $v\mapsto v^\top A_Iv-2f^\top v$, completing the square gives $\hat u_p^\top A_I\hat u_p-2f^\top\hat u_p=-u_p^\top A_Iu_p+\lVert\hat u_p-u_p\rVert_{A_I}^2$. Neither $\tr\Lambda$ nor $u_p^\top A_Iu_p$ depends on $\theta$. ∎

The label-free objective is the discrete Ritz principle: training minimises the energy of the network's own fields, as in the Deep Ritz method [39], and here this is provably the same as supervised training in the energy norm. It removes the classical solves from data generation; labelled examples remain necessary for validation, because the objective's value contains the unknown constant. In floating-point arithmetic the two objectives are not identical: the gradient of $\mathcal L_{\text{en}}$ involves the difference $A_I\hat H-BQ$, which cancels to the size of the error. Which of the two trains better at equal cost is a registered comparison of Stage 0.

Time steps. A network that approximates the exact evolution of the solution over a time interval should satisfy the semigroup property: one step of length $2\Delta t$ equals two steps of length $\Delta t$. For such networks, which appear at Stage 2 for flow, a penalty on the difference is added to the objective, as row S2 suggests. The tier-0 networks approximate the implicit Euler step instead, which does not have this property, since $(I+2\Delta t\,L)^{-1}\neq(I+\Delta t\,L)^{-2}$ for a nonzero operator $L$; for them the penalty is omitted, and the declared range of $\Fo$ is covered by exact examples.

Stages. Training proceeds by family, each stage with its own gate (Table 6).

Training from scratch and fine-tuning. Stages 0 and 1 train small networks from scratch: the families are linear or nearly so, examples are inexpensive, and small networks are what the cost requirement S7 demands. A chart maps every piece onto the uniform grid that pretrained models accept, which makes fine-tuning them possible for the first time in this setting. Stage 2 therefore includes a comparison at equal compute between (i) a network trained from scratch, (ii) a pretrained model, DPOT [26], Walrus [27] or GPhyT [28], fine-tuned by low-rank adaptation with new input layers for the chart fields, and (iii) the model of (ii) compressed into the size of (i) by training (i) to reproduce its outputs. Poseidon is excluded because its licence does not permit the intended use (row S12). The risks of the fine-tuning arm are its inference cost and the possibility that pretraining, which never saw a metric or a boundary input, does not transfer.

7Cost and training time

All measurements in this section were made in one cloud container with four processor cores; its wall times are not those of the owner's laptop, so only ratios between methods are quoted as results. Every estimate is labelled as such and shows its arithmetic.

The coupling probe. The script scripts/arch_coupling_cost.py solves the problem that charts produce in reference coordinates: a $P_x\times P_y$ arrangement of square pieces, each with $n\times n$ cells, a smooth log-normal conductivity, zero boundary values on the outer boundary and a smooth source, steady and with implicit steps at $\Fo=1,0.1,0.01$ per piece. Exact classical subdomain solves stand in for the networks. This is the best case for any learned subdomain solver: no network can converge in fewer rounds than its exact counterpart under the same scheme, so the round counts are lower bounds for learned ones. Iterations run to a relative residual of $10^{-8}$. The strategies are restricted additive Schwarz with an overlap of two cells (RAS), the scheme used by most of the learned methods of Section 9; the non-overlapping Robin exchange \eqref{eq:robin} (OSM, for optimised Schwarz method), with $p$ set to half the textbook optimum after one tuning run; each of these accelerated by GMRES or by Anderson acceleration combining the last five iterates; a two-level method with the coarse functions $\{1,\xi,\eta,\xi\eta\}$ per piece; exact substructuring; and the port-reduced superelement with $m$ cosine modes per side.

Figure 6 shows the round counts. On $8\times8$ pieces, RAS needs 1,864 rounds, the Robin exchange 1,124, with Anderson acceleration 152, with GMRES 83, and the two-level method 70. Exponents fitted between $2\times2$, $4\times4$ and $8\times8$ pieces are $0.9$ to $1.05$ in the number of pieces $P$ for the one-level iterations, $0.26$ to $0.5$ with GMRES, and $0.14$ to $0.29$ with the coarse space. The superelement forms its port matrices from $4m$ subdomain solves per piece and performs no rounds. Wall times (Table 7) give a different picture: a monolithic sparse direct solve of the whole problem is faster than every decomposed method in every case, by a factor of about two over the best iterative coupling on $8\times8$ pieces. This agrees with the finding of [40] that their learned two-level solver is about twice as slow as a parallel direct solver.

Port truncation. The superelement with exact modes differs from the exact solution only by the truncation of the interface to $m$ modes per side (Table 8). The error falls about tenfold for each doubling of $m$; at $m=16$ it is between $1.7\times10^{-4}$ and $1.4\times10^{-3}$, below the accuracy a network is likely to reach, so it is unlikely to be the limiting term.

Forward cost of the network. The script scripts/arch_backbone_timing.py times untrained U-shaped networks of the architecture of Section 4.4 at three widths, with 65 output fields (Table 9, Figure 7). Untrained weights cost exactly as much as trained ones, so these are the forward costs of the design; they say nothing about accuracy.

When a learned superelement pays. One forward pass of a network with one to two million parameters costs 8 to 17 ms per piece at $n=64$, against 0.85 ms for a classical subdomain solve with a stored factorisation: the network is 9 to 20 times more expensive per call. Its advantage is the number of calls. A tier-0 solve needs one forward pass per piece, whereas the iterative couplings need one call per piece per round; on $8\times8$ pieces that is 64 calls against $70\times64=4{,}480$ for the two-level method and $1{,}864\times64\approx1.2\times10^5$ for RAS. Against the classical superelement the comparison depends on whether its constraint modes can be reused. Recomputing one piece's port matrix classically at $n=64$ costs about 21 ms of factorisation and 47 ms of subdomain solves, 68 ms in total measured; the network replaces this by one forward pass, 4 to 9 times cheaper. When the geometry and the coefficients do not change, however, the classical modes are computed once and cost nothing afterwards, and no network can compete. The learned superelement therefore pays where the classical work cannot be reused or does not scale: when geometry, material or parameters change between solves, as in design optimisation and uncertainty quantification; when the local physics is expensive, so that a classical subdomain solve takes seconds; and in three dimensions, where a monolithic direct solve becomes unaffordable.

An estimate for one steady solve. estimate Combining measured call counts with measured per-call costs, on $8\times8$ pieces of $32\times32$ cells a tier-0 solve with the medium network would take $64\times(2.7\text{ to }6.5)$ ms, about 0.2 to 0.4 s, plus an interface solve of a few milliseconds; the same network inside the two-level iteration would take $4{,}480\times(2.7\text{ to }6.5)$ ms, about 12 to 29 s. The monolithic direct solve of the same problem took 0.8 s. These figures use this container's processor; neither a graphics processor nor the owner's laptop has been measured.

Training cost. estimate Data generation costs, per example at $n=64$, one factorisation and 65 solves, about $21+65\times0.94\approx82$ ms from the measured components above, so $10^5$ examples need about 2.3 core-hours; the label-free objective of Corollary 11 removes this cost except for validation examples. For training we assume, as is typical, that a forward and backward pass costs about three forward passes. On this container's processor the medium network would then need about $3\times7.7$ ms per example, or roughly 64 hours for 100 passes over $10^5$ examples, which is why Stage 0 is planned on a rented graphics processor, where the cost is expected to be a few hours of graphics-processor time (GPU-hours). 9Related work and what is new

Learned subdomain solvers in Schwarz iterations. Schwarz Neural Inference [36] trains a transformer, an architecture whose attention layers weight every input point against every other, on random polygons and composes it by overlapping additive Schwarz iteration with Dirichlet exchange; its convergence theorem is discussed in Remark 10. Neural-Schwarz tiling [41] applies a network defined on the graph of grid nodes to patches of cubic cells; without a coarse space its iteration count grows from 4 to 61 as the resolution increases, and its speed-up over a single-core finite-element solver falls from 111 to 3.2. The learning-based domain decomposition method of [42] uses square subdomains related by affine maps and proves an existence result for the alternating method. Mosaic flows [43] compose networks trained on unit squares and impose Dirichlet data exactly by a lifting. SNAP-DDM [44] and DD-DeepONet [45] use Robin exchange with learned subdomain solvers. Mao and Fan [40] use a learned preconditioner inside a Krylov solve on each subdomain, with a two-level Robin method whose coarse space is built by probing the learned solvers; their solver is about twice as slow as a parallel direct solver. A non-overlapping hybrid of finite elements and a neural operator [46] reports iteration counts comparable to the purely finite-element coupling (26 against 25). All of these exchange values or Robin data and iterate. None returns constraint modes, and none forms the interface stiffness from the network's fields.

Learned components of the decomposition method. Taghibakhshi et al. [47] learn the Robin interface matrices of optimised Schwarz methods with a loss that estimates the spectral radius of the iteration, so that training targets the iteration count. HINTS [48] interleaves a neural operator with classical relaxation sweeps of Jacobi or Gauss–Seidel type, which remove the components the network represents poorly. A survey of learning in domain decomposition is [49].

Geometry. DIMON [50] transports problems to a reference domain and encodes the shape by principal components of landmark displacements. The diffeomorphism neural operator [51] maps each domain to a generic one by a discrete harmonic map and gives the network the physical coordinates sampled on the generic grid. Geo-FNO [32] learns the deformation itself, without a guarantee of injectivity. GINO [52] and Transolver [53] work on point clouds and meshes without a map. A benchmark of topological generalisation [33] observes that a diffeomorphism cannot create a tunnel or a cavity and that the error of neural operators tracks the frequency content of the input. None of these passes the pulled-back coefficient of each piece of a decomposition to the network.

Learned Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps and local stiffness. Boutilier, Brenner and Miguez [54] learn local Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps of nonlinear elliptic problems and assemble them in a substructured Newton solve. Ling, Ying and Zhou [55] split a Dirichlet-to-Neumann map into an analytic principal part and a learned remainder. Kröpfl, Maier and Peterseim [56] train networks that output compressed local stiffness matrices. Wang et al. [57] and Zhang and Jiang [58] learn multiscale basis functions and reduced models. Among classical methods, the constraint modes of Craig and Bampton [11], the multiscale finite element method [12] and static condensation with port reduction [13, 14] are the direct ancestors of the output format. Wave-domain splitting with a checkable non-expansiveness condition has been developed for classical lumped subsystems described by energy-based (port-Hamiltonian) models [35]; training on derivatives of the solution operator is developed in [59]; and energy-based training of networks in [39].

10Limitations

  1. Cost in two dimensions. At the sizes measured, a monolithic sparse direct solve beats every decomposed method, and stored classical constraint modes beat any network on a fixed geometry. The design pays only where classical work cannot be reused or does not scale (Section 7), and gate E5 may fail.
  2. Nothing has been trained. Whether the general input class can be learned to useful accuracy at a forward cost below the classical alternatives is unknown; comparisons C1 and C5 address it.
  3. Three dimensions. No injectivity theorem for harmonic charts exists there, and decomposing a solid into topological cubes is an open problem of mesh generation. The fallback is a point- or mesh-based network that returns the same port data. Interface systems in three dimensions call for preconditioned conjugate gradients with a coarse space instead of a direct solve; this is untested.
  4. Nonlinear and hyperbolic problems. Tier 1 carries only a local guarantee, and a shock that crosses an interface is outside the scope of the design.
  5. Non-matching parametrisations. With the mortar transfer the method is nonconforming, and Theorem 6 does not cover it.
  6. Corners of the port modes (Remark 4), and cross-points of iterative tier-2 schemes formulated on vertices; the face-based formulation used here has none.
  7. The block layout that cuts an arbitrary domain into four-sided blocks is not built.
  8. Measurements come from one machine, which is not the owner's laptop, and the specification of Table 1 from one network.

AThe Expert Card specification

Every subdomain solver, learned or classical, ships with a manifest, the Expert Card, that the host reads before admitting it. The values below are placeholders that show the format; each certificate is measured, dated, and names its method. THERM is the project's interface type for heat transfer, whose two conjugate quantities are temperature and heat flux; the host refuses to connect interfaces of different types.

expert: chart-diffusion-2d
version: 0.1.0
licence: <chosen by the owner>                 # S12
family: diffusion
ports: [THERM]                                  # typed port of the project's port algebra
chart:
  reference: unit-square
  methods: [conformal-equal-value, winslow-arc-length, transfinite, imported]
  certificate: bilinear-positive-jacobian + simple-boundary
  inputs: [logJ_centred, re_mu, im_mu, log_kappa, source]
  envelope: {sup_abs_mu: k_mu, logJ_range: l_J, smoothness: s, kappa_range: [kappa_min, kappa_max]}

  port_basis: cosine
  port_modes_per_side: 16

  principal_part: plain-square
  particular_field: true

certificates:
  gram_identity_residual: {value: ..., date: ...}                 # gate SE1

The host applies the following rules when it reads a card.

  1. A chart outside the envelope is refused, and the violated quantity is named; the layout may then cut the piece further.
  2. A Fourier number outside fourier_range is refused (row S2).
  3. Tier 0 is admitted when the output format is constraint-modes and the physics is linear; the guarantee of Theorem 5 then holds without further certificates.
  4. At tier 2, a certified contraction factor below one on the fine space admits the convergence claim of Theorem 9; without it the interface is admitted without a convergence claim.
  5. A missing certificate is reported, never assumed.

BDerivations

The metric in terms of the Beltrami coefficient. With $F_\xi=F_z+F_{\bar z}$ and $F_\eta=i(F_z-F_{\bar z})$, and $\mu=F_{\bar z}/F_z$,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:metric-derivation} \begin{gathered}|F_\xi|^2=|F_z|^2|1+\mu|^2,\qquad |F_\eta|^2=|F_z|^2|1-\mu|^2,\\ \langle F_\xi,F_\eta\rangle=\operatorname{Re}\bigl(F_\xi\overline{F_\eta}\bigr)=2|F_z|^2\operatorname{Im}\mu,\end{gathered} \end{equation}

so $G=|F_z|^2\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}|1+\mu|^2 & 2\operatorname{Im}\mu\\ 2\operatorname{Im}\mu & |1-\mu|^2\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. The identity $|1+\mu|^2|1-\mu|^2-4(\operatorname{Im}\mu)^2=(1-|\mu|^2)^2$ gives $\det G=|F_z|^4(1-|\mu|^2)^2=J^2$, and inverting $G$ and multiplying by $J=|F_z|^2(1-|\mu|^2)$ gives \eqref{eq:metric-mu}.

Proof of Proposition 2. Let $L=DF$ at the corner, and let $e_1,e_2$ be the directions of the two sides of the square there. Since $F$ is continuously differentiable up to the corner, the sides of the piece leave the image of the corner in the directions of $Le_1$ and $Le_2$, so $Le_1=a\,u_1$ and $Le_2=b\,u_2$ with $a,b\gt0$ and unit vectors $u_1,u_2$ that enclose the interior angle $\alpha$. The quadrant spanned by $e_1,e_2$ is mapped onto the convex cone spanned by $u_1,u_2$, which shows $\alpha\lt\pi$. Now

\begin{equation}\label{eq:corner-proof} \begin{gathered}L^\top L=\begin{pmatrix}a^2 & ab\cos\alpha\\ ab\cos\alpha & b^2\end{pmatrix},\\ K+\frac1K=\frac{\sigma_1^2+\sigma_2^2}{\sigma_1\sigma_2}=\frac{\tr(L^\top L)}{\sqrt{\det(L^\top L)}}=\frac{a^2+b^2}{ab\sin\alpha}\ge\frac{2}{\sin\alpha},\end{gathered} \end{equation}

where $\sigma_1\ge\sigma_2$ are the singular values of $L$ and $K=\sigma_1/\sigma_2$. Since $t\mapsto t+1/t$ is increasing on $[1,\infty)$, $K\ge K_0$, where $K_0+1/K_0=2/\sin\alpha$, that is, $K_0=(1+|\cos\alpha|)/\sin\alpha$. This equals $\cot(\alpha/2)$ for $\alpha\le\pi/2$ and $\tan(\alpha/2)$ for $\alpha\ge\pi/2$, which is \eqref{eq:corner}. Equality holds when $a=b$. ∎

The conformal chart at a corner. Near a corner of interior angle $\alpha$, a conformal chart behaves like $w=\zeta^{2\alpha/\pi}$, which opens the right angle of the square at $\zeta=0$ to the angle $\alpha$. Its Jacobian is $J=|dw/d\zeta|^2=(2\alpha/\pi)^2|\zeta|^{4\alpha/\pi-2}$, and with $r=|w|=|\zeta|^{2\alpha/\pi}$ this is a constant multiple of $r^{\,2-\pi/\alpha}$.

The principal part on the square. For the bottom side, with boundary values $\cos(k\pi\xi)$ there and zero on the other three sides, the continuum principal part of the steady problem is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:principal} H_k^{(0)}(\xi,\eta)=\sum_{j\ge1}c_{jk}\,\sin(j\pi\xi)\,\frac{\sinh\bigl(\omega_j(1-\eta)\bigr)}{\sinh\omega_j},\qquad c_{jk}=\begin{cases}\dfrac{2j\,\bigl(1-(-1)^{j+k}\bigr)}{\pi\,(j^2-k^2)}, & j\ne k,\\[1ex] 0, & j=k,\end{cases} \end{equation}

with $\omega_j=j\pi$. For one implicit step the equation on the square is $-\hat\Delta\hat u+\Fo^{-1}\hat u=0$, and the same formula holds with $\omega_j=\sqrt{j^2\pi^2+\Fo^{-1}}$; as $\Fo\to0$ the profile $\sinh(\omega_j(1-\eta))/\sinh\omega_j\approx e^{-\omega_j\eta}$ decays within a distance of order $\sqrt\Fo$ of the side, as in Figure 4. The coefficients $c_{jk}$ are those of the sine series of $\cos(k\pi\xi)$ on $(0,1)$, which converges in $L^2$ but not at the endpoints, where the boundary values jump (Remark 4). The principal parts used by the network are the discrete counterparts of \eqref{eq:principal}, computed exactly for the finite-volume scheme by a fast sine transform.
