1The five-minute tour

In short

Atlas wants to simulate whole engineered systems, such as a rocket or a racing car, by cutting them into many small pieces and letting a learned expert solve each piece. That fails in practice for a precise reason: today's learned solvers were never designed to be pieces of something larger. This page designs one that is. The guiding rule is that the network proposes and the host decides: the network suggests a handful of candidate fields for its piece, and the coupling software, which knows the exact equations, picks the best combination and enforces every law that holds across pieces. That rule is what lets us prove, rather than hope, that a learned piece cannot break the assembly.

1.1The problem in one paragraph

Most interesting simulations are of things that are big, curved and made of different materials: a nozzle that is metal on one side and burning gas on the other, a battery pack, a wing that bends in the air flowing past it. The standard way to compute such things is domain decomposition: cut the domain into pieces, solve each piece, and make the pieces agree where they touch. A trained neural network is an attractive replacement for the solver of each piece, because one network can serve thousands of similar pieces at a fixed cost per evaluation. This project measured what happens when a large pretrained network is actually used that way (Section 3). It could not accept curved pieces, it had no input for the values its neighbours imposed on it, and its reaction to small changes at its boundary was subtly wrong, so the procedure that makes neighbours agree slowed down or stalled. The fault was not the network's accuracy. It was that nothing about it was designed to be one piece of a larger whole.

1.2The idea in one picture

The design rests on four separations of responsibility, which Figure 1 follows on one test geometry:

  1. Topology goes to the decomposition. The domain is cut into pieces that each have four sides, so the network never sees a hole, a fork or a loop.
  2. Geometry goes to a classical chart. Each piece is mapped onto the unit square by a map whose correctness is checked exactly. The curved shape then reappears as a "material property" of the square, so the network always works on the same square grid.
  3. Physics goes to the network. The network answers one question about the square: given what happens on the four sides, what happens inside? It answers by returning a small set of fields, one per pattern of boundary values.
  4. Agreement goes to the host. The coupling software, called the host, evaluates the exact energy of the network's fields, assembles all pieces, and solves for the combination in which neighbouring pieces agree. For linear problems this is a single solve with no iteration.

1.3What is guaranteed, in one sentence

For linear, symmetric physics such as heat conduction or elasticity: whatever the network returns, the assembled system can be solved, no piece can appear softer than it really is, and the answer is the best that the network's fields can represent. Errors in a piece enter the assembly squared, so a 1% error in the fields costs about 0.01% in each piece's stiffness. This is proved on paper here and machine-checked in Lean machine-checked. For other kinds of physics the guarantee changes shape but does not disappear (Section 5).

1.4What changed in this version

Beyond linear physics

The network becomes a trial-space generator: it proposes candidate fields, and the host projects the true equations onto them. The guarantee is then set by the structure of the physics, in five classes from G0 (linear, symmetric) to G3 (shock-capturing flow). Section 5.

Different physics at one seam

Air against a rocket's skin: only what crosses the skin must match, which is power. Each side declares typed ports (force and velocity, temperature and heat), and the host connects matching ports. Section 6.3.

Different fields on one piece

A temperature expert and a stress expert on the same frame couple through a host-owned "bond" that is provably well posed for any expert output. Section 6.4.

A catalog of experts

Twelve families of chart operators cover about forty PDEs, because experts are distinguished by the kind of boundary data they accept, not by the name of the PDE. Sections 7 and 8.

Speed from the square

Because every piece is the same square, fast transforms apply: a preconditioned solve in 30 iterations at every resolution, cheaper output heads, and low-precision networks that cost almost nothing in accuracy. Section 9.

A two-sided certificate

In two dimensions the same network, run on the reciprocal material, gives a guaranteed lower bound on each piece's stiffness. Together with the upper bound this brackets the truth. Section 10.

1.5What has been measured so far

No network has been trained. Every measurement below uses exact classical solves, sometimes deliberately perturbed to stand in for a network of a given accuracy.

1.6What is not yet known

  • Whether the networks can be trained to useful accuracy over the range of shapes the charts produce. That is Stage 0 of the training plan (Section 11) open.
  • Whether it is faster. In two dimensions, at the sizes measured, a single direct solve of the whole problem beats every decomposed method, classical or learned. A learned piece pays where classical work cannot be reused or does not scale: changing shapes, expensive local physics, three dimensions (Section 12) open.
  • Three dimensions, shocks that cross a seam, and several design questions listed in Section 15.

2Background, from the ground up

In short

This section explains every method the design builds on, assuming linear algebra, multivariable calculus and some PDE theory but no domain decomposition and no machine learning. The four ideas to take away: (1) a piece's behaviour as seen from its boundary is a symmetric matrix, its stiffness; (2) pieces can be made to agree either by iterating, which needs many rounds, or by condensing each piece to its boundary stiffness and solving once; (3) a curved piece is equivalent to a square with a modified material; (4) a neural network is a fitted function, and a neural operator is a fitted function from fields to fields.

2.1The running example: heat conduction and its energy

The picture

Hold the edges of a metal plate at fixed temperatures and wait. The temperature inside settles into the arrangement that is "as smooth as the edges allow": among all temperature fields with those edge values, it is the one with the least total squared gradient, weighted by the conductivity. This is the Dirichlet principle, and almost everything below is a consequence of it. A soap film spanning a bent wire frame does the same thing with area.

Let $\Omega\subset\R^2$ be a bounded domain and $\kappa$ a conductivity: at each point a symmetric, positive definite $2\times2$ matrix (a scalar times the identity for an isotropic material). The model problem is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:model} \sigma u-\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla u)=f\quad\text{in }\Omega,\qquad u=g\quad\text{on }\partial\Omega, \end{equation}

with $\sigma\ge0$. For $\sigma=0$ it is steady heat conduction. For $\sigma=c/\Delta t$ it is one step of the implicit (backward) Euler method for $c\,\partial_tu-\nabla\cdot(\kappa\nabla u)=s$, with heat capacity $c$, time step $\Delta t$ and $f=s+c\,u^{\text{old}}/\Delta t$. The Fourier number of a step on a region of size $L$,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:fourier} \Fo=\frac{\kappa\,\Delta t}{c\,L^{2}}, \end{equation}

measures the step in units of the region's diffusion time. Heat spreads a distance of about $\sqrt{\kappa\Delta t/c}$ in one step, so for $\Fo\ll1$ a step only changes the solution in a thin layer next to any change, and for $\Fo\gg1$ a step behaves like a steady solve.

The energy of a temperature field $v$ is $a(v,v)$, where

\begin{equation}\label{eq:energy} a(v,w)=\int_\Omega\bigl(\kappa\nabla v\cdot\nabla w+\sigma\,v\,w\bigr)\,dx,\qquad \lVert v\rVert_a=a(v,v)^{1/2}. \end{equation}

The weak solution satisfies $a(u,w)=\int fw$ for every $w$ that vanishes on the boundary, and among all $v$ with boundary values $g$ it minimises $\tfrac12a(v,v)-\int fv$. In particular, with $f=0$ the solution is the least-energy field with the given boundary values, and it is $a$-orthogonal to every field that vanishes on the boundary. That orthogonality is the key step of every proof in Section 4.6.

The discrete version. We discretise by cell-centred finite volumes on a grid of cells: the unknowns are cell values, and the heat flowing between two neighbouring cells is a conductance times the difference of their values. The result is a symmetric positive definite matrix $A$ with $v^\top Av$ the discrete energy. Split the unknowns into interior ones ($I$) and boundary ones ($\Gamma$). The discrete Dirichlet principle is then

\begin{equation}\label{eq:schur} \min_{v_I}\begin{pmatrix}v_I\\ g\end{pmatrix}^{\!\top}\!\begin{pmatrix}A_{II} & A_{I\Gamma}\\ A_{\Gamma I} & A_{\Gamma\Gamma}\end{pmatrix}\!\begin{pmatrix}v_I\\ g\end{pmatrix}=g^\top Sg,\qquad S=A_{\Gamma\Gamma}-A_{\Gamma I}A_{II}^{-1}A_{I\Gamma}, \end{equation}

with the minimum at $v_I=-A_{II}^{-1}A_{I\Gamma}g$. The matrix $S$, the Schur complement, is symmetric positive semidefinite, because $g^\top Sg$ is a minimum of a nonnegative quantity.

Why it matters

$S$ is everything the outside world needs to know about the piece: it maps the temperatures you impose on the boundary to the heat that flows through it. It is the piece's stiffness as seen from its boundary. Once you have it, the interior can be forgotten until you want to look inside.

2.2Cutting a domain into pieces

Divide $\Omega$ into non-overlapping pieces $\Omega_1,\dots,\Omega_P$. The solutions $u_i$ on the pieces form a solution of the whole problem exactly when, on every shared side $\Gamma_{ij}$, two transmission conditions hold:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:transmission} u_i=u_j,\qquad (\kappa\nabla u_i)\cdot n_i+(\kappa\nabla u_j)\cdot n_j=0\qquad\text{on }\Gamma_{ij}. \end{equation}

In words: the temperature is continuous, and the heat leaving one piece enters the other. ($n_i$ is the outward normal of piece $i$.)

The Dirichlet-to-Neumann map $\Lambda_i$ of a piece takes boundary values $\lambda$, solves the source-free problem inside with those values, and returns the normal heat flux on the boundary. By Green's identity, $\langle\Lambda_i\lambda,\nu\rangle=a_i(w_\lambda,w_\nu)$, with $w_\lambda$ the solution for boundary values $\lambda$, so $\Lambda_i$ is symmetric and positive semidefinite. For $\sigma=0$ its null space is the constants: a uniform temperature carries no flux. In the discrete setting $\Lambda_i$ is exactly the Schur complement $S_i$ of the piece.

Take the common boundary value $\lambda$ on all shared sides as the unknown. The flux-balance condition becomes one linear system for $\lambda$:

\begin{equation}\label{eq:interface} \Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^{P}R_i^\top\Lambda_iR_i\Bigr)\lambda=\chi, \end{equation}

where $R_i$ picks out the part of $\lambda$ on piece $i$'s boundary and $\chi$ collects the flux imbalance caused by the sources. This is just finite-element assembly, one level up: each piece acts like one big element whose stiffness is $\Lambda_i$. Solving \eqref{eq:interface} and then recovering each interior is called substructuring or static condensation.

2.3Two ways to make the pieces agree

The picture

Imagine a row of people each holding one section of a long rope that must hang in one smooth curve. In the iterative approach each person adjusts their section to match their neighbours' current ends, then everyone looks again, round after round, until nobody needs to move. In the condensed approach each person first reports how stiff their section is at its two ends; one person then computes where all the junctions must be, and everybody hangs their section accordingly. The second needs more work up front and no rounds at all.

Iterating: Schwarz methods. Forming $\Lambda_i$ costs one piece solve per boundary unknown. Iterative methods avoid this and only ever solve pieces with given boundary data, repeating until the transmission conditions hold. One solve on every piece is a round.

  • Schwarz's alternating method [1] uses overlapping pieces and exchanges values. Restricted additive Schwarz (RAS) is its common parallel form [2].
  • The diameter bound. In one round information moves only from a piece to its neighbours, but in a steady problem every point depends on the data everywhere. So any neighbour-to-neighbour method needs at least as many rounds as there are pieces across the domain, and round counts grow with the number of pieces.
  • Robin exchange. Without overlap, exchanging values alone does not converge. Lions [3] showed that exchanging a combination of value and flux does: in round $r+1$ piece $i$ solves with
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:robin} (\kappa\nabla u_i^{r+1})\cdot n_i+p\,u_i^{r+1}=-(\kappa\nabla u_j^{r})\cdot n_j+p\,u_j^{r}\qquad\text{on }\Gamma_{ij}. \end{equation}
    Optimised Schwarz methods choose $p$ to converge fastest [4]. For the Laplacian, an error that oscillates along the interface with wavenumber $k$ shrinks per exchange by $\rho(k,p)=\bigl((p-|k|)/(p+|k|)\bigr)^2$, which is why one value of $p$ cannot be good for all wavenumbers.
  • Coarse spaces repair the diameter bound by adding, each round, a small global correction built from a few functions per piece (for example $1,\xi,\eta,\xi\eta$). Round counts then become almost independent of the number of pieces [5].
  • Krylov and Anderson acceleration. For a linear problem one round is an affine map $x\mapsto Tx+c$. The generalised minimal residual method (GMRES) [6] searches the space spanned by repeated applications of $I-T$ for the iterate with the smallest residual. Anderson acceleration [7, 8] does something similar using only past iterates, with no derivatives.

Condensing: superelements with port modes. Static condensation becomes cheap when the boundary values are restricted to a few patterns. On each side, parametrised by $s\in[0,1]$, use the cosines $q_k(s)=\cos(k\pi s)$, $k=0,\dots,m-1$, as port modes; collected over the four sides they form a matrix $Q$ with $4m$ columns, and a side's boundary values are $\lambda=Qc$ for a vector $c$ of port coordinates. The constraint mode $H_k$ is the field inside the piece whose boundary values are exactly the $k$-th port mode, with every other mode held at zero. The term comes from structural dynamics, where Craig and Bampton [9] used such modes to couple substructures of aircraft; the same objects are the basis functions of the multiscale finite element method [10] and of port-reduced static condensation [11, 12]. The piece's port matrix is its Dirichlet-to-Neumann map restricted to port modes,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:portmatrix} \Lambda^{(m)}=Q^\top S\,Q\in\R^{4m\times4m}, \end{equation}

and a piece represented only by its port matrix and a load vector, assembled as if it were one finite element, is a superelement. Forming it costs $4m$ piece solves; afterwards the coupling is one small system with no rounds.

Example

With 16 modes per side, a piece talks to its neighbours through 64 numbers instead of its full boundary. On an $8\times8$ arrangement of pieces that is 1,792 interface unknowns, and the error from keeping only 16 modes was between $1.7\times10^{-4}$ and $1.4\times10^{-3}$ in the probes measured, below what a network is likely to reach.

2.4Maps between shapes: why a curved piece is a square with a different material

The picture

Draw a square grid on a rubber sheet and stretch the sheet over a curved piece. Heat now has to flow along squashed and sheared grid cells. You can describe exactly the same physics on the unstretched square, provided you say that the square's "material" conducts better along some directions and worse along others, in just the way the stretching distorted it. Geometry becomes anisotropy. The network then only ever has to solve problems on the square.

Let $\hat\Omega=[0,1]^2$ be the reference square with coordinates $(\xi,\eta)$. A chart of a piece is a continuous bijection $F:\hat\Omega\to\overline{\Omega_i}$, continuously differentiable inside, orientation-preserving, with Jacobian determinant $J=\det DF>0$, carrying the square's four sides onto the piece's four sides. With $\hat u=u\circ F$, the chain rule and the change of variables give

\begin{equation}\label{eq:pullback} \int_{\Omega_i}\bigl(\kappa\nabla u\cdot\nabla v+\sigma uv\bigr)dx=\int_{\hat\Omega}\bigl(\mathsf A\,\hat\nabla\hat u\cdot\hat\nabla\hat v+J\hat\sigma\,\hat u\hat v\bigr)d\hat x,\qquad \mathsf A=J\,DF^{-1}\,\hat\kappa\,DF^{-\top}. \end{equation}

So the problem on the curved piece is the same problem on the square, with conductivity $\mathsf A$ and capacity and source multiplied by $J$. The flux through any part of the boundary is the same number in both descriptions. Seen from its boundary, a piece is characterised only through $\mathsf A$ [13]: two pieces of different shape whose pulled-back coefficients agree are indistinguishable from outside.

The Beltrami coefficient: how much a chart squashes. Identify $\R^2$ with $\mathbb C$ and write $F_z=\tfrac12(F_\xi-iF_\eta)$, $F_{\bar z}=\tfrac12(F_\xi+iF_\eta)$. The Beltrami coefficient and the dilatation are

\begin{equation}\label{eq:beltrami} \mu=\frac{F_{\bar z}}{F_z},\qquad K=\frac{1+|\mu|}{1-|\mu|}. \end{equation}

Intuitively, the chart maps a tiny circle to a tiny ellipse; $K$ is the ratio of the ellipse's axes, and the argument of $\mu$ says which way it points. A map with $\mu=0$ is conformal: it preserves angles and maps tiny circles to tiny circles. With $G=DF^\top DF$ the chart's metric, a short computation (Appendix B) gives

\begin{equation}\label{eq:metric-mu} J\,G^{-1}=\frac{1}{1-|\mu|^{2}}\begin{pmatrix}|1-\mu|^{2} & -2\operatorname{Im}\mu\\ -2\operatorname{Im}\mu & |1+\mu|^{2}\end{pmatrix},\qquad\det\bigl(J\,G^{-1}\bigr)=1, \end{equation}

so for an isotropic conductivity $\mathsf A=\hat\kappa\,JG^{-1}$ depends on $\hat\kappa$ and $\mu$ alone. The eigenvalues of $JG^{-1}$ are $K$ and $1/K$: the chart makes the square conduct $K^2$ times better in one direction than the other.

Three ways to build a chart.

  • Conformal charts. Every four-cornered region can be mapped conformally onto a rectangle $[0,M]\times[0,1]$, corners to corners, for a unique $M$, its conformal modulus [14]. Squeezing that rectangle into the unit square gives a chart with the constant $\mu=(M-1)/(M+1)$; a piece with modulus near 1 has $\mathsf A\approx\hat\kappa I$, the easiest possible input.
  • Harmonic (Winslow) charts [15] solve Laplace's equation for $\xi$ and $\eta$ on the piece and invert. The Radó–Kneser–Choquet theorem guarantees a valid map in 2-D [16], a guarantee that fails in 3-D [17].
  • Transfinite interpolation [18] blends the four side curves by an explicit formula, with no solve and no guarantee.

2.5Neural networks and neural operators, briefly

The picture

A neural network is a very flexible formula with millions of adjustable numbers. "Training" means adjusting those numbers until the formula's outputs match examples, the way one fits a line to data points, only in millions of dimensions. A neural operator is such a formula whose input and output are whole fields sampled on a grid: here the input is the piece's material and shape, and the output is the temperature fields inside.

  • A neural network composes affine maps with a fixed nonlinear function applied entry by entry (here GELU, $x\Phi(x)$ with $\Phi$ the normal distribution function [19]). Its parameters $\theta$ are fitted by minimising a loss, an average discrepancy over examples, by stochastic gradient descent. One evaluation is a forward pass.
  • A neural operator maps functions to functions and can be applied at different grid resolutions [20]. The Fourier neural operator [21] multiplies the Fourier coefficients of its input by learned factors; the convolutional neural operator [22] composes convolutions at several resolutions.
  • A convolution with a $3\times3$ kernel replaces each grid value by a learned weighted sum of it and its eight neighbours. A U-shaped network [23] applies convolutions at successively coarser then finer grids with shortcuts between equal resolutions, so that every output can depend on the whole input, as the solution of an elliptic problem must. Scalar inputs such as the Fourier number enter by FiLM [24], which rescales and shifts each feature channel by amounts computed from the scalars.
  • Foundation models are large networks pretrained on many solved PDE datasets, such as Poseidon [25], DPOT [26], Walrus [27] and GPhyT [28], meant to be adapted by fine-tuning (continued training on a small dataset), for example by low-rank adaptation [29].

3What went wrong before: the measured requirements

In short

The project ran a long campaign with a frozen pretrained network (Poseidon-T [25]) as the piece solver inside coupled flow simulations. It recorded twelve properties a learned piece solver needs and that network lacked (Table 2). The two lessons that shape everything here: the failures were mostly about the network's interface to the coupled solver (shape, boundary input), not its training; and the coupling's speed is set by how a piece reacts to changes at its boundary, not by how accurate it is on average.

Three terms recur in the table. A window is a rectangular region handed to the network. The slow band is the set of error patterns the coupling iteration removes most slowly. Defect correction is an iteration in which the network acts as an approximate inverse and the classical residual corrects it, so the network can change the speed of convergence but not the answer.

4The core design: four separations of responsibility

In short

This section builds the linear version of the chart operator, version 1 of the design, which is the corner of the general form (Section 5) where the strongest guarantee holds. The decomposition cuts the domain into four-sided pieces chosen so their charts are nearly undistorted. Each chart is checked exactly for validity. The network is asked a single question about the unit square and answers with constraint modes. The host then forms each piece's stiffness from those modes in a way that is provably safe (Section 4.6).

4.1Topology goes to the decomposition

The picture

A network that works on a square cannot see holes or forks, so it should never be shown one. Cut a ring into arcs, a T-junction into three channels and a junction block, and every piece is a "curved square": four sides, four corners. Within a channel, cutting along lines of equal "distance along" and "distance across" makes every piece look almost exactly like a square, so its chart barely distorts anything.

Every piece is a quadrilateral: a region bounded by a closed curve with four marked corners. Within a channel-like block with a start edge, an end edge and two walls, the layout software computes two harmonic coordinates. The along coordinate $u$ solves $\Delta u=0$ with $u=0$ on the start edge, $u=1$ on the end edge and $\partial_nu=0$ on the walls. The across coordinate $v$ is the same with the roles of edges and walls exchanged. The block is cut along level curves at equally spaced values.

Proposition 1 (Equal-value cuts.)

Let a block have conformal modulus $M$. For integers $n,m\ge1$, $0\le k\lt n$, $0\le r\lt m$, the piece $\{k/n\le u\le(k+1)/n,\ r/m\le v\le(r+1)/m\}$ has modulus $M'=Mm/n$. Its chart has the constant Beltrami coefficient $\mu=(M'-1)/(M'+1)$, so $J\,G^{-1}=\operatorname{diag}(1/M',M')$, and all four corners of an interior piece are right angles.

In plain words: cutting at equal values of the harmonic coordinates produces pieces whose charts are as undistorted as their proportions allow, and choosing the number of cuts $n\approx Mm$ makes every piece nearly a perfect square.

Show the proof

Let $\Phi$ be the conformal map of the block onto $[0,M]\times[0,1]$ sending the start edge to $\{0\}\times[0,1]$ and the walls to the horizontal sides. $\operatorname{Re}\Phi$ is harmonic, equals $0$ and $M$ on the edges, and has zero normal derivative on the walls (by the Cauchy–Riemann equations, since $\operatorname{Im}\Phi$ is constant there). By uniqueness of this mixed problem $\operatorname{Re}\Phi=Mu$, and likewise $\operatorname{Im}\Phi=v$. The piece is therefore the preimage of a rectangle with side ratio $(M/n)/(1/m)=M'$, and conformal maps preserve the modulus. The inverse chart is $F=\Phi^{-1}\circ L$ with $L(\xi,\eta)=(M(\xi+k)/n,(\eta+r)/m)$; since $\Phi^{-1}$ is conformal, $\mu_F=\mu_L=(M'-1)/(M'+1)$, and \eqref{eq:metric-mu} gives $J\,G^{-1}$. Level curves of $u$ meet the walls at right angles because $\partial_nu=0$, and level curves of $u$ and $v$ meet at right angles because $\Phi$ is conformal. ∎

On the s-channel ($M=8.872$) nine pieces along give a predicted modulus of 0.986; the moduli computed independently on each piece lie between 0.977 and 0.988 measured. On a three-armed bend ($M=1.731$), a $5\times3$ cut predicts 1.039 and gives 1.038 to 1.067. Equal modulus is not equal area: the nine s-channel pieces hold 398 to 698 grid cells, yet each costs one network call on the same reference grid. Building such block layouts automatically for arbitrary domains is a separate, unbuilt part of the project (Section 16).

4.2Geometry goes to a certified chart

What the network receives. By \eqref{eq:pullback} and \eqref{eq:metric-mu}, a piece with isotropic conductivity is fully described by $\hat\kappa$, $\mu$ and $J$. So the network receives, on an $n\times n$ reference grid,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:inputs} \bigl(\log J-\overline{\log J},\ \operatorname{Re}\mu,\ \operatorname{Im}\mu,\ \log\hat\kappa,\ \hat f\bigr)\quad\text{and the scalars}\quad(\Fo,\ \text{physical parameters}), \end{equation}

where $\overline{\log J}$ is the mean of $\log J$ and the Fourier number uses the piece's area as $L^2$. Translating, rotating or uniformly scaling a piece changes none of these inputs, so the network never has to learn those symmetries; and $|\mu|\lt1$ always, which makes it a better-behaved input than the entries of $\mathsf A$, which blow up as $|\mu|\to1$.

The picture: corners force distortion

A square's corners are right angles. If a piece has a $60^\circ$ corner, a chart that is smooth up to that corner must squash the square there, because a smooth map sends a right angle to a right angle only if it is locally a rotation and scaling. Either the chart distorts angles (large $|\mu|$) or it lets the cell sizes blow up or collapse at the corner (large range of $J$). You cannot avoid both.

Proposition 2 (Distortion at a corner.)

Let $F$ be continuously differentiable up to a corner of the square with $DF$ nonsingular there, and let the image corner have interior angle $\alpha$. Then $\alpha\lt\pi$, and the dilatation there satisfies

\begin{equation}\label{eq:corner} K\ \ge\ \max\Bigl(\tan\frac\alpha2,\ \cot\frac\alpha2\Bigr). \end{equation}

The proof (Appendix B) is about the singular values of the linear map that sends a right angle to the angle $\alpha$. A conformal chart has $K=1$ and escapes the bound only by being singular at the corner: there $J\sim r^{2-\pi/\alpha}$, unbounded at acute corners and vanishing at obtuse ones. The s-channel's end piece has corners of $68^\circ$ and $148^\circ$, which force $K\ge1.48$ and $K\ge3.49$. Table 3 and Figure 3 compare three charts of that piece.

Re-entrant corners ($\alpha\gt\pi$, a corner pointing into the piece) are where the solution itself is singular, behaving like $r^{\pi/\alpha}$ with unbounded gradient. Here the conformal chart is a gift: it pulls the singular function $\operatorname{Im}w^{\pi/\alpha}$ back to the polynomial $2\xi\eta$. The singularity moves into $J$, which is integrable, and the network is asked to represent a smooth function.

The certificate. A computed chart is the set of images $X_{ij}$ of the reference grid's nodes, interpolated bilinearly on each cell. Whether it is a valid, non-folding map can be checked exactly and cheaply:

Proposition 3 (Discrete chart certificate.)

Let $F$ be the bilinear interpolant of nodal values $X_{ij}$. If (a) the Jacobian determinant of $F$ is positive at the four corners of every cell, and (b) the boundary nodes, in order, form a simple closed polygon with positive orientation, then $F$ is a homeomorphism of the closed square onto the closed region bounded by that polygon.

In plain words: check that no cell is flipped or degenerate and that the outline does not cross itself; then the whole map is guaranteed one-to-one. The check costs one pass over the grid.

Show the proof

On a cell, $F=\mathbf p+\mathbf b\xi+\mathbf c\eta+\mathbf d\xi\eta$, so $\det DF=\mathbf b\times\mathbf c+\xi(\mathbf b\times\mathbf d)+\eta(\mathbf d\times\mathbf c)$; the $\xi\eta$ term vanishes because $\mathbf d\times\mathbf d=0$. The determinant is affine on the cell, hence positive throughout if positive at its corners, and $F$ is a local diffeomorphism inside each cell. Neighbouring cells share an edge on which both interpolants are the same linear function, and their images lie on opposite sides of it. At an interior node the four image angles lie in $(0,\pi)$ and close up, so they sum to a multiple of $2\pi$ less than $4\pi$, hence to exactly $2\pi$: the images do not overlap near the node. So $F$ is a local homeomorphism. For a point off the boundary polygon, the number of preimages equals the polygon's winding number about it (the degree is computed from the boundary), which by (b) is 1 inside and 0 outside. A continuous bijection from a compact space to a Hausdorff space is a homeomorphism. ∎

Analogous guarantees exist for discrete harmonic maps on triangulations [30] and for curved elements in 3-D [31]. The certificate would also accept a chart produced by a network, but learned charts [32] are not used: the pull-back \eqref{eq:pullback} is exact for every valid chart, so nothing about the map needs learning.

The envelope. Each trained network declares the range of inputs on which it is trusted,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:envelope} \sup|\mu|\le k_\mu,\qquad \max\log J-\min\log J\le\ell_J,\qquad \lVert\hat\nabla\log J\rVert_\infty+\lVert\hat\nabla\mu\rVert_\infty\le s, \end{equation}

plus ranges for $\hat\kappa$ and $\Fo$. The smoothness bound is there because neural-operator errors track the frequency content of their inputs [33]. The host measures every chart and refuses one outside the envelope, naming the violated quantity; the layout can then cut the piece again. Charts are computed from the smooth outline as drawn, never from the workbench's staircase mask, and the classical reference solver runs in chart coordinates on the same grid. That gives a reference of the network's own kind at any resolution (row S8 of Table 2).

4.3Physics goes to the network: constraint modes

The picture

Ask every network the same single question: "if the four sides of the square are held at these values, what is the field inside?" Instead of answering for one particular set of side values, the network answers for each of the 64 basic patterns (16 wiggles per side), returning 64 fields. Any boundary condition the host later needs is a combination of those patterns, so the answer for it is the same combination of the 64 fields. Walls, insulated sides, convective cooling and neighbours are all the host's business, expressed as relations among the 64 coordinates. No network can invent its own conventions for boundary conditions.

The discrete energy of a piece. On the reference grid with $n\times n$ cells, a piece's unknowns are the cell values $u\in\R^{n^2}$ and the values $\lambda\in\R^{4n}$ on its boundary faces. Its discrete energy is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:piece-energy} \mathcal E(u,\lambda)=\begin{pmatrix}u\\ \lambda\end{pmatrix}^{\!\top}\!M\begin{pmatrix}u\\ \lambda\end{pmatrix},\qquad M=\begin{pmatrix}A_I & -B\\ -B^\top & D\end{pmatrix}, \end{equation}

a sum of squares: interior conductances times squared differences of neighbouring cells, boundary half-cell conductances times squared differences between a cell and its face, and the capacity $J\hat\sigma$ times squared cell values. $M$ is symmetric positive semidefinite, and $A_I$ is positive definite.

Exact constraint modes. With the port basis $Q\in\R^{4n\times4m}$, the exact constraint modes, particular field and port matrix are

\begin{equation}\label{eq:modes} \begin{gathered}H=A_I^{-1}BQ\in\R^{n^2\times4m},\qquad u_p=A_I^{-1}f,\qquad V=\begin{pmatrix}H\\ Q\end{pmatrix},\\ \Lambda=V^\top MV=Q^\top\bigl(D-B^\top A_I^{-1}B\bigr)Q.\end{gathered} \end{equation}

By the Dirichlet principle, $H_k$ is the least-energy interior field paired with the boundary values $Qe_k$, and $u_p$ is the response to the source with zero boundary values.

What the network returns.

\begin{equation}\label{eq:learned} \hat H_k=H_k^{(0)}+\beta\odot N_{\theta,k}(z),\qquad \hat u_p=\beta\odot N_{\theta,p}(z),\qquad \beta(\xi,\eta)=16\,\xi(1-\xi)\,\eta(1-\eta). \end{equation}

Here $z$ are the inputs \eqref{eq:inputs}, $N_{\theta,k}$ and $N_{\theta,p}$ are output channels of one network, and $\odot$ multiplies entry by entry. $H^{(0)}_k$ is the principal part: the exact constraint mode of the plain square (unit conductivity, the given Fourier number), computed once per resolution by a fast sine transform in $O(n^2\log n)$ operations. One forward pass returns all $4m+1=65$ fields.

Why a principal part and a bubble
  • The network only has to learn the difference between the actual piece and the plain square. Equal-value cuts make that difference small, so the network represents a small correction instead of the whole field (whether this makes learning easier is tested at Stage 0).
  • The boundary values are the separate vector $\lambda=Qe_k$, so every field the network returns has exactly the prescribed boundary values; it cannot violate them. The bubble $\beta$, which is zero on the boundary and one at the centre, additionally makes the correction fade towards the boundary, as the true correction does.
Remark 4 (Port modes at corners.)

The cosine modes of two adjacent sides are independent, so the boundary values of one mode jump at a corner. In the continuum such boundary values have infinite energy; on the grid the energy is finite but grows like $\log n$. A conforming alternative, sines that vanish at corners plus four corner functions, is registered as comparison C6. Section 10 gives a second reason to prefer it.

Symmetry. The square's eight symmetries (rotations and reflections) act on the inputs and permute the port modes, and the exact solution operator commutes with them. For example, rotating the reference coordinates by $90^\circ$ replaces $\mu$ by $-\mu$. At Stage 0 the symmetry is imposed by training on all eight copies of each example.

4.4The network itself

The network maps the five input fields and the scalars to $4m+1$ output fields. Because the constraint modes solve an elliptic problem, every output must depend on every input, so the network needs a view of the whole square. Because the output format already guarantees what the coupling needs (Section 4.6), the network itself needs no certified property, such as a bound on its sensitivity, and its architecture is free.

The default is a U-shaped convolutional operator in the manner of [22]: four resolution levels, two $3\times3$ convolutions per level with GELU, averaging to coarsen and bilinear interpolation to refine, skip connections, and FiLM on the scalars. Because the square's opposite sides are unrelated, inputs are extended by mirror reflection and outputs by odd reflection, not periodically. The registered alternative is a spectral operator in the square's sine and cosine bases. The default size is about $10^6$ parameters, with $n=64$ and training mixing $n=32$, 64 and 128.

4.5Interface data, conservation and time

  • Interface data. At the linear tier, pieces exchange port coordinates: 16 cosine coefficients per side. Two charts sharing a side parametrise it differently, so the host transfers coordinates between them by an $L^2$ projection in physical arc length (a mortar). Each network carries about 20 modes per side internally so that the 16 shared modes survive this transfer; the 20 is an estimate to be checked.
  • Conservation. For conservation laws, a second output format returns fluxes $\hat{\mathbf F}$ on the faces of the reference grid, and the update is their exact discrete divergence,
    \begin{equation}\label{eq:conservative} J\,\frac{\hat u^{\,\text{new}}-\hat u^{\,\text{old}}}{\Delta t}=-\hat\nabla_h\cdot\hat{\mathbf F}+J\hat s, \end{equation}
    so the conserved quantity balances exactly in every cell and every side sends its neighbour the flux it used itself.
  • Time. The step enters as the piece's Fourier number. Each network declares the range of $\Fo$ it was trained on, and the host refuses a call outside it, so row S2's failure becomes a declared refusal instead of a silent error. At fixed step and geometry the modes do not change, so each later step costs one forward pass (the new particular field) and one back-substitution.

4.6Agreement goes to the host: the energy Gram and its guarantee

In short

The host does not ask the network for each piece's stiffness. It computes the stiffness itself, as the exact energy of the network's fields: a Gram matrix. Because the network's fields have exactly the right boundary values and the host uses the true energy, the computed stiffness can only err on one side (too stiff, never too soft), by exactly the energy of the network's errors, which is quadratic in them. The assembled system is therefore always solvable, and its answer is the best the network's fields can represent. All of this is machine-checked in Lean.

The picture

Recall the soap film: the true film has the least energy of all surfaces with the same frame. Any other surface with the same frame has more energy, never less, and if it differs from the true film by a small bump of size $\varepsilon$, its extra energy is of size $\varepsilon^2$, because the true film is at a minimum and a minimum is flat to first order. The network's constraint modes are such "other surfaces": same frame, slightly wrong interior. So the stiffness the host computes from them is slightly too large, by a second-order amount, and never too small. A direct guess of the stiffness has no such protection: a small error in a guessed matrix can make it indefinite, which means a piece that releases energy instead of storing it.

Assembly. Let the pieces be $i=1,\dots,P$, with energy matrices $M_i$, port bases $Q_i$, sources $f_i$ and network outputs $\hat H_i,\hat u_{p,i}$. The global unknown is a vector $c$ of port coordinates, one block per side, shared by the two pieces at that side; $R_i$ picks piece $i$'s $4m$ coordinates. With $\tilde V_i=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}\hat H_i\\ Q_i\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ the host computes

\begin{equation}\label{eq:gram} \tilde\Lambda_i=\tilde V_i^\top M_i\tilde V_i,\qquad \tilde b_i=\hat H_i^\top f_i-\tilde V_i^\top M_i\begin{pmatrix}\hat u_{p,i}\\ 0\end{pmatrix}, \end{equation}

assembles and solves

\begin{equation}\label{eq:assembled} \tilde{\mathsf S}\,c=\tilde\chi,\qquad \tilde{\mathsf S}=\sum_{i=1}^PR_i^\top\tilde\Lambda_iR_i,\qquad \tilde\chi=\sum_{i=1}^PR_i^\top\tilde b_i, \end{equation}

and reconstructs each piece as

\begin{equation}\label{eq:reconstruct} \tilde u_i=\hat u_{p,i}+\hat H_iR_ic,\qquad \lambda_i=Q_iR_ic. \end{equation}

The reconstruction combines fields the network already returned, so a linear steady solve costs one forward pass per piece. Replacing $\hat H_i,\hat u_{p,i}$ by the exact $H_i,u_{p,i}$ gives the classical superelement, whose solution is $u_m$ and interface matrix $\mathsf S$. For symmetric matrices, $X\succeq Y$ (the Loewner order) means $X-Y$ is positive semidefinite.

Theorem 5 (The Gram port matrix.)

Let $\hat H_i$ be arbitrary and $E_i=\hat H_i-H_i$ the error of the network's modes. Then:

  1. $\tilde\Lambda_i$ is symmetric positive semidefinite, and positive definite whenever $M_i$ is.
  2. $\tilde\Lambda_i-\Lambda_i=E_i^\top A_{I,i}E_i$. Hence $\tilde\Lambda_i\succeq\Lambda_i$, $\lVert\tilde\Lambda_i-\Lambda_i\rVert_2\le\lVert A_{I,i}\rVert_2\lVert E_i\rVert_2^2$, and $\tr\tilde\Lambda_i-\tr\Lambda_i=\sum_k\lVert E_{i,k}\rVert_{A_{I,i}}^2$.
  3. $\tilde{\mathsf S}\succeq\mathsf S$. If $\mathsf S$ is positive definite, so is $\tilde{\mathsf S}$, with $\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{\mathsf S})\ge\lambda_{\min}(\mathsf S)$, and \eqref{eq:assembled} has a unique solution for every network output.

In plain words: no output can make a piece singular, indefinite or softer than it is; the excess stiffness is exactly the energy of the field errors; so a 1% field error gives an error of order $10^{-4}$ in the stiffness. machine-checked Atlas.Piece.gram_posSemidef, Atlas.tier0_headline

Show the proof

(i) $x^\top\tilde\Lambda_ix=(\tilde V_ix)^\top M_i(\tilde V_ix)\ge0$. If $M_i$ is positive definite, $\tilde V_ix=0$ forces $Q_ix=0$ and so $x=0$, since $Q_i$ has orthonormal columns.

(ii) Write $\tilde V_i=V_i+\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}E_i\\ 0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$. Expanding $\tilde\Lambda_i=\tilde V_i^\top M_i\tilde V_i$ gives $V_i^\top M_iV_i$, two cross terms, and $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}E_i\\0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)^\top M_i\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}E_i\\0\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)=E_i^\top A_{I,i}E_i$. The cross terms contain the interior rows of $M_iV_i$, which are $A_{I,i}H_i-B_iQ_i=0$ by the definition of $H_i$: this is the discrete Dirichlet principle, "the exact mode is energy-orthogonal to every interior perturbation". So they vanish.

(iii) $\tilde{\mathsf S}-\mathsf S=\sum_iR_i^\top(\tilde\Lambda_i-\Lambda_i)R_i$ is a sum of positive semidefinite matrices, and the smallest eigenvalue is monotone in the Loewner order by the Rayleigh quotient. ∎

The setting for optimality. Let $U$ be the set of global discrete fields (cell values on every piece and face values on every boundary face) on which neighbouring pieces agree about shared faces and the prescribed boundary values hold. Let $U_0$ be the same with zero prescribed values, and define

\begin{equation}\label{eq:global-energy} \mathcal E(w)=\sum_{i=1}^P\Bigl(\tfrac12\,w_i^\top M_iw_i-f_i^\top w_{I,i}\Bigr),\qquad \lVert w\rVert_M^2=\sum_{i=1}^Pw_i^\top M_iw_i . \end{equation}

The minimiser $u$ of $\mathcal E$ over $U$ is the ordinary finite-volume solution of the whole problem. Assume that the problem is well posed (so $\lVert\cdot\rVert_M$ is a norm on $U_0$), that shared sides are parametrised identically by both charts, and that the prescribed boundary values lie in the span of the port modes. Then the trial set of everything the network's fields can represent,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:trialset} \tilde W=\Bigl\{w\in U:\ w_i=\begin{pmatrix}\hat u_{p,i}\\ 0\end{pmatrix}+\tilde V_iR_ic\ \text{ for some }c\Bigr\}, \end{equation}

lies inside $U$.

Theorem 6 (Energy optimality.)

Under these assumptions, the reconstruction $\tilde u$ satisfies

\begin{equation}\label{eq:cea} \lVert u-\tilde u\rVert_M=\min_{w\in\tilde W}\lVert u-w\rVert_M . \end{equation}

Moreover, with $c^\star$ the port coordinates of the classical superelement solution $u_m$ and $e_{p,i}=\hat u_{p,i}-u_{p,i}$,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:cea-bound} \lVert u-\tilde u\rVert_M\ \le\ \lVert u-u_m\rVert_M+\Bigl(\sum_{i=1}^P\bigl\lVert e_{p,i}+E_iR_ic^\star\bigr\rVert_{A_{I,i}}^2\Bigr)^{1/2}. \end{equation}

In plain words: the coupled answer is the best possible combination of the network's fields, measured in energy (Céa's lemma [34] with constant one); its error splits into the truncation of the interface to 16 modes plus the network's own errors, weighted by how much each mode is actually used. machine-checked Atlas.Superelement.energy_optimal, Atlas.Superelement.hostSystem_existsUnique

Show the proof

For $w\in U$, $w-u\in U_0$ and the first variation of $\mathcal E$ at its minimiser vanishes on $U_0$; since $\mathcal E$ is quadratic, $\mathcal E(w)=\mathcal E(u)+\tfrac12\lVert w-u\rVert_M^2$. On $\tilde W$, parametrised by $c$, the gradient of $\mathcal E$ is $\tilde{\mathsf S}c-\tilde\chi$, so \eqref{eq:assembled} says $\tilde u$ minimises $\mathcal E$, hence $\lVert u-\cdot\rVert_M$, over $\tilde W$. For the bound, the element of $\tilde W$ with coordinates $c^\star$ differs from $u_m$ by cell values $e_{p,i}+E_iR_ic^\star$ and zero face values, whose $M_i$-norm is the $A_{I,i}$-norm; use the triangle inequality and \eqref{eq:cea}. ∎

On the assumptions. When the two charts of a shared side parametrise it differently, the mortar makes the two pieces agree only in the projected sense. The method then becomes nonconforming: Theorem 5 still holds piece by piece, but \eqref{eq:cea} acquires a consistency term that has not been analysed open. Boundary values outside the port span are replaced by their projection, which adds to the truncation error.

Measured: Gram against a direct stiffness head

The probe arch_superelement_gram.py takes $4\times4$ square pieces of $32\times32$ cells, a smooth random conductivity and 16 modes per side, computes exact modes and port matrices, and perturbs both by the same relative amount $\varepsilon$, standing in for a network of that accuracy. Table 5 and Figure 5 show the outcome.

  • The Gram port matrices err by about $0.9\varepsilon^2$ and stay positive semidefinite; the identity of Theorem 5(ii) holds to $2\times10^{-15}$.
  • The direct head's matrices are indefinite for 3 or more of 16 pieces at every $\varepsilon$, because a steady piece's exact stiffness is singular (a uniform temperature carries no flux), so any small symmetric error can make it indefinite.
  • At $\varepsilon=0.01$ the coupled error is 0.27% with the Gram and 9.2% with the direct head measured.
Proposition 7 (Perturbation of the interface solve.)

If $\mathsf S,\tilde{\mathsf S}$ are symmetric positive definite, $\mathsf Sc=\chi$ and $\tilde{\mathsf S}\tilde c=\tilde\chi$, then

\begin{equation}\label{eq:perturb} \lVert\tilde c-c\rVert_2\le\frac{\lVert\mathsf S-\tilde{\mathsf S}\rVert_2\,\lVert c\rVert_2+\lVert\chi-\tilde\chi\rVert_2}{\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{\mathsf S})}, \end{equation}

and for the Gram, $\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{\mathsf S})\ge\lambda_{\min}(\mathsf S)$ and $\lVert\mathsf S-\tilde{\mathsf S}\rVert_2\le\sum_i\lVert A_{I,i}\rVert_2\lVert E_i\rVert_2^2$. machine-checked Atlas.interface_perturbation

Show the proof

$\tilde{\mathsf S}(\tilde c-c)=(\tilde\chi-\chi)+(\mathsf S-\tilde{\mathsf S})c$; multiply by $\tilde{\mathsf S}^{-1}$, whose norm is $1/\lambda_{\min}(\tilde{\mathsf S})$, and use $\lVert R_i\rVert_2\le1$. ∎

4.7When the Gram is not available: tiers and a contraction contract

In short

Some experts cannot return constraint modes: a pretrained donor network, a classical code, a black box. Those must be coupled by iteration, and an iteration of learned maps can fail to converge even when every map is accurate (Remark 10). The remedy is a checkable property, a certified contraction factor, rather than accuracy. Version 1 organised this into three tiers; Section 5 replaces the tiers by guarantee classes, but the iterative tier and its theorem remain the route for black boxes.

The picture: waves at a joint

At a shared side, split what crosses into an incoming wave and an outgoing wave, like the two directions of traffic on a road. A piece that only stores or dissipates energy (a passive piece) can never send out more wave power than it receives. So exchanging waves between passive pieces never amplifies anything. That is the classical reason Robin exchange is stable, and the property one must certify for a learned piece used as a black box.

On a side, let $e$ be the boundary temperatures and $f=\Lambda e$ the flux into the piece. For an impedance $Z\gt0$ define the incoming and outgoing waves

\begin{equation}\label{eq:waves} a=\frac{e+Zf}{2\sqrt Z},\qquad b=\frac{e-Zf}{2\sqrt Z},\qquad\text{so that}\qquad\langle e,f\rangle=\lVert a\rVert^2-\lVert b\rVert^2 . \end{equation}

The power entering the piece is incoming minus outgoing wave power. At the solution, a piece's incoming wave is its neighbour's outgoing wave, which is the Robin condition \eqref{eq:robin} with $p=1/Z$. A source-free piece maps incoming to outgoing waves by its scattering map, the Cayley transform of its Dirichlet-to-Neumann map,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:cayley} \mathcal S=(I-Z\Lambda)(I+Z\Lambda)^{-1}. \end{equation}
Lemma 8 (Passive pieces do not amplify waves.)

If $\Lambda$ is symmetric positive semidefinite and $Z\gt0$, then $\lVert\mathcal S\rVert_2\le1$. If moreover $\langle\Lambda e,e\rangle\ge\gamma\lVert e\rVert^2$ and $\lVert\Lambda\rVert_2\le\Gamma$, then $\lVert\mathcal S\rVert_2^2\le1-4Z\gamma/(1+Z\Gamma)^2$. machine-checked Atlas.cayley_bound

Show the proof

$I+Z\Lambda$ has eigenvalues at least one. For $y=(I+Z\Lambda)e$, $\lVert(I-Z\Lambda)e\rVert^2=\lVert y\rVert^2-4Z\langle\Lambda e,e\rangle\le\lVert y\rVert^2$. Under the further hypotheses $4Z\langle\Lambda e,e\rangle\ge4Z\gamma\lVert y\rVert^2/(1+Z\Gamma)^2$, since $\lVert y\rVert\le(1+Z\Gamma)\lVert e\rVert$. ∎

A Gram expert therefore also serves the iterative tier: its scattering map is non-expansive for every impedance, by Theorem 5(i) and Lemma 8. For a genuine black box nothing of the kind is automatic, and the guarantee available is this classical result:

Theorem 9 (Perturbed contraction.)

Let $(X,d)$ be complete, $T:X\to X$ have a fixed point $a^\star$, and $\tilde T$ satisfy $d(\tilde Ta,\tilde Ta')\le\rho\,d(a,a')$ with $\rho\lt1$ and $d(\tilde Ta,Ta)\le\delta$ for all $a$. Then $\tilde T$ has a unique fixed point $\tilde a^\star$, reached from every start at rate $\rho^r$, and

\begin{equation}\label{eq:perturbed-contraction} d(\tilde a^\star,a^\star)\le\frac{\delta}{1-\rho}. \end{equation}

In plain words: convergence of the learned iteration is a checkable property of the network (its contraction factor), and accuracy enters only through how far apart the learned and classical answers end up. machine-checked Atlas.contraction_contract, Atlas.two_level_contract, Atlas.tier2_headline

Show the proof

Existence, uniqueness and the rate are Banach's fixed-point theorem. Then $d(\tilde a^\star,a^\star)\le d(\tilde T\tilde a^\star,\tilde Ta^\star)+d(\tilde Ta^\star,Ta^\star)\le\rho\,d(\tilde a^\star,a^\star)+\delta$; rearrange. ∎

Remark 10 (Accuracy is not enough: a counterexample.)

A published convergence theorem for learned Schwarz iterations [35] assumes that (a) the classical map contracts and (b) the learned solver is uniformly within $c$ of the exact one, and concludes convergence. Take one subdomain, exact solver $S(u)=0$, classical map $F(u)=(1-\tau)u$, and learned solver $\tilde S(u)=-c\tanh(ku)$, so $|\tilde S-S|\le c$. With $\tau=\tfrac12$, $c=1$, $k=10$ the learned map $\tilde F(u)=(1-\tau)u+\tau\tilde S(u)$ has its only fixed point at 0 with $\tilde F'(0)=-4.5$, which repels; from $u_0=1$ the iterates settle on the two-cycle $\pm0.332471$ measured arch_sni_counterexample.py. Both hypotheses hold and the conclusion fails; the hypothesis $\Lip(\tilde F)\lt1$ of Theorem 9 restores it.

A sensitivity (Lipschitz) bound for a trained network can be certified by bound propagation [36], which has been formalised in Lean [37], but such bounds are loose for large networks. The fallback is architectural: a classical scattering map of known contraction plus a small learned correction with a certified bound. Wave-domain coupling that is energy-safe after any number of exchanges is developed in [38].

5The general form: beyond linear physics

In short

Version 1 looked as if it depended on the PDE being linear. It does not. Its guarantee rests on two facts that make sense for any PDE: (E) every field the network returns has exactly the right boundary values, and (H) the host, never the network, evaluates the physics. Keeping those two and dropping everything else gives the general form. The network becomes a trial-space generator: it proposes a few candidate fields. The host projects the true equations onto them: it minimises the true energy where one exists, and the true residual where none does. What can be guaranteed then depends on the structure of the physics, in five classes G0 to G3.

5.1Where version 1 is linear, and what its guarantee really rests on

Read the proof of Theorem 5 again: the only facts it uses are that the network's fields have exact traces (E), and that the host computes the energy itself (H). Positivity and optimality then follow from the Dirichlet principle: the exact solution minimises the energy among fields with the same boundary values. Linearity only made that minimum a quadratic form.

5.2The network as a trial-space generator

The picture

Do not ask the network for the answer; ask it for a small set of good directions to look in, each with the right boundary values. The host, which knows the equations exactly, finds the best answer in that set. For a linear problem the set contains the exact answer, and one search suffices. For a nonlinear problem the host searches, moves to the best point found, asks the network for fresh directions there, and searches again, the way Newton's method takes repeated steps.

On a piece, the physics is given to the host as a discrete residual on the reference grid, $R(w;z)=0$ with $w=(u,\lambda)$: cell values $u$ of every field of the family, and boundary values $\lambda$ per typed port component (Section 6.3). The host can evaluate $R$ and its directional derivatives without solving anything. A generalised chart operator is a network

\begin{equation}\label{eq:generator} \mathcal N_\theta:\ (z,x)\ \longmapsto\ \bigl(\hat H(x),\ \hat B(x),\ \hat u(x)\bigr), \end{equation}

where $x$ is the current state (absent for a linear family), $\hat H$ are state-dependent constraint modes, one per port coordinate, $\hat B$ are $r$ interior enrichment modes with zero trace, and $\hat u$ is a predicted interior field. Together they define the affine trial set

\begin{equation}\label{eq:affine-trial} \widetilde W(x)=\Bigl\{\bigl(\hat u+\hat Hc+\hat Ba,\ Qc\bigr):\ c\in\R^{p},\ a\in\R^{r}\Bigr\}, \end{equation}

every member of which has the exact trace $Qc$. Neighbouring pieces share the port coordinates $c$ of their common sides. The host solves the projected problem on the assembled trial set, updates $x$, and calls the network again if the physics is nonlinear. Version 1 is the linear corner: modes independent of $x$, $\hat u$ the particular field, $r=0$, one call and one solve.

Why affine and not curved

One could let the network return a field as a nonlinear function of the boundary data (a "manifold" trial set), which needs fewer numbers. The affine form keeps the host's projected problem convex whenever the physics' energy is convex, because a convex function composed with an affine map is convex. The projected problem is then uniquely solvable and Newton's method on it converges globally. The manifold form gives both up proposal.

5.3The guarantee classes G0 to G3

The class is read off the structure of the physics, not chosen. What each class guarantees holds for every network output: accuracy is the network's business, consistency the host's.

G0′: non-symmetric physics needs a second set of modes

The picture

With a flow carrying heat downstream, "how the piece responds to a push at the inlet" is not the mirror image of "how much a measurement at the outlet depends on the interior". The Gram trick used one set of fields for both roles. The non-symmetric version needs two: the network's primal modes (how the piece responds) and its adjoint modes (how a boundary measurement weights the interior). For advection the adjoint is the same physics with the flow reversed, so one network can produce both.

Proposition 11 (Petrov–Galerkin identity.)

Let $M=\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}A_{II}&A_{I\Gamma}\\A_{\Gamma I}&A_{\Gamma\Gamma}\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ be non-symmetric, with primal modes $V=(H;Q)$, $A_{II}H+A_{I\Gamma}Q=0$, and adjoint modes $W=(G;Q)$, $A_{II}^\top G+A_{\Gamma I}^\top Q=0$. Then $W^\top MV=\Lambda$, and for learned $\tilde V=V+(E_V;0)$, $\tilde W=W+(E_W;0)$,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:pg} \tilde W^\top M\tilde V-\Lambda=E_W^\top A_{II}E_V . \end{equation}

The error is the product of the primal and adjoint errors, so it stays quadratic. Using the primal modes alone leaves a first-order cross term. derived

Show the proof

Expand $\tilde W^\top M\tilde V$. The cross terms are $E_W^\top(A_{II}H+A_{I\Gamma}Q)$ and $(A_{II}^\top G+A_{\Gamma I}^\top Q)^\top E_V$, both zero by the definitions of the modes; the remaining term is $E_W^\top A_{II}E_V$. With $\tilde W=\tilde V$ the second cross term does not vanish, because $V$ does not satisfy the adjoint equation. ∎

Measured

Upwind advection–diffusion on one $32\times32$ piece, 16 cosine modes per side (arch_general_form_checks.py): the port error's slope against the field error was 1.97, 1.98 and 2.00 with adjoint modes at Péclet numbers 0, 20 and 200, against 1.96, 1.12 and 0.99 with primal modes alone. The identity held to $4\times10^{-16}$. The finding to keep: the symmetric part of the exact port matrix was indefinite at Péclet 20 and 200 (smallest eigenvalue $-4.4\%$ and $-14.7\%$ of the largest). Positivity is not lost by the network; it was never there, because an inflow boundary feeds the piece whatever it is given. Passivity must come from exchanging the advected flux explicitly (Section 6.1) measured.

G1: convex energies, and a learned step that is never worse than a classical one

The picture

Many nonlinear problems still minimise an energy; it is just not quadratic (heat conduction when conductivity depends on temperature; a magnet that saturates; a metal that yields). Every field with the right boundary values then has at least the minimum energy, so the host can score any proposal honestly. If the host always includes, alongside the network's proposals, one ordinary classical step, then the best point it finds is at least as good as that classical step, whatever the network proposed. The network can make the method faster; it cannot make a step worse.

Proposition 12 (Nonlinear Ritz over learned modes.)

Let the discrete energy $E$ be $m$-strongly convex and $L$-smooth, with minimiser $u^\star$ over the conforming fields $U$, and let $\widetilde W\subset U$.

  1. Upper bound: the piece energy computed over the trial set is at least the true one at the same boundary data, the nonlinear form of $\tilde\Lambda\succeq\Lambda$.
  2. Quasi-optimality: $\tilde u=\arg\min_{\widetilde W}E$ satisfies $\lVert\tilde u-u^\star\rVert\le\sqrt{L/m}\,\min_{w\in\widetilde W}\lVert w-u^\star\rVert$. For a quadratic energy in its own norm, $L=m$: version 1's constant one.
  3. Subspace correction: if each outer step's trial set contains the current iterate and one classical descent direction besides the network's proposals, the energy decreases monotonically and each step is at least as good as one classical step from the same point.

derived

Show the proof

(1) Every trial field has the exact trace, so it is admissible in the minimisation that defines the true piece energy. (2) Since $\nabla E(u^\star)$ vanishes on directions in $U_0$, strong convexity and smoothness give

$$\tfrac m2\lVert\tilde u-u^\star\rVert^2\le E(\tilde u)-E(u^\star)=\min_{w\in\widetilde W}\bigl[E(w)-E(u^\star)\bigr]\le\tfrac L2\min_{w\in\widetilde W}\lVert w-u^\star\rVert^2 .$$

(3) The classical step from the current point lies in the trial set, so the minimum over the trial set is at most its energy. This is subspace correction in the sense of [39]. ∎

Measured, and the correction it forced

On $-\Delta u+100u^3=0$ on one $32\times32$ piece, the "network" proposed Newton's direction with a smooth error of relative size $\varepsilon$:

  • Outer iterations were 6, 7, 11, 25, 48 and 154 for $\varepsilon=0$, 0.01, 0.1, 0.5, 1 and 3, against 923 for the classical direction alone. The energy decreased in every run and no step was worse than a classical step from the same point (arch_general_form_checks.py) measured.
  • An uninformative random proposal never did worse than a classical step at any of its points, yet ended slower than the plain classical run, whose own trajectory happened to be faster. The honest statement is "never worse than the classical worst case", not "never slower than classical".

A nonlinear route that needs no new network. When the nonlinearity sits in a coefficient, as in conduction with $\kappa(T)$, each fixed-point (Picard or Kačanov) step is a linear problem with a frozen coefficient field, exactly what the linear expert is trained on [40]. So the linear expert already serves this nonlinear class through its coefficient input, and every inner solve keeps the machine-checked guarantee.

G2: no energy, so project the residual, certify it, and fall back

With no energy, the host minimises the norm of the true residual over the trial set: the least-squares Petrov–Galerkin (LSPG) projection of nonlinear model reduction [41]. Near a regular solution, with $C_s=\lVert DR(u^\star)^{-1}\rVert$ and $C_L$ a local Lipschitz bound,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:lspg} \lVert\tilde u-u^\star\rVert\ \le\ C_s\,\lVert R(\tilde u)\rVert_*\ \le\ C_sC_L\min_{w\in\widetilde W}\lVert w-u^\star\rVert . \end{equation}

The middle quantity is computed by the host at every call: a certificate of the expert's accuracy at that call. If it is above tolerance, classical Newton–Krylov continues from the network's answer, and the final answer is the classical one (defect correction). The constant $C_s$ is not certified, so G2 has a certificate but no a-priori bound. For incompressible flow the velocity modes should be stream-function modes, so every trial field is divergence-free. Domain-decomposed versions of LSPG exist for classical reduced models [42, 43], including on learned nonlinear manifolds [44].

G3: explicit hyperbolic steps

For shock-forming flow the network proposes face fluxes (the conservative format \eqref{eq:conservative}), so conservation is exact. The host blends each proposed flux toward a classical first-order flux with the largest weight that keeps every cell's new state physically admissible: positive density and pressure, a local maximum principle. This is flux-corrected transport and convex limiting [45, 46, 47]. It gives positivity and conservation for any network output. Entropy stability, the hyperbolic analogue of passivity, needs entropy-stable fluxes in addition [48].

5.4The five slots of a chart operator

In short

Every expert in Atlas is five choices, and the families of Section 8 differ only in these:

  1. chart information: what the network is shown;
  2. trial space: what kind of boundary data the PDE takes, and which modes fill the space;
  3. ports and conservation: what crosses a seam, and what is conserved exactly;
  4. host contract: the class G0 to G3;
  5. backbone and time: the network architecture and how time enters.

Slot 1: what the chart shows the network

The picture

When a piece is mapped to the square, different kinds of quantity transform differently. A temperature is a number at a point, so it simply moves with its point. A heat flux is a rate of flow through a surface, so it must be re-measured through the deformed surface. A density is an amount per unit area, so it is rescaled by the local change of area. The PDE decides which kind each quantity is, and the chart must present it in that form.

Three consequences derived:

  • In two dimensions geometry and anisotropy are the same thing to a diffusion operator. The operator sees only $\mathsf A$, and $\det\mathsf A=\det\kappa$ for every chart: the chart changes only the shape of $\mathsf A$, two numbers. An anisotropic material on an undistorted chart and an isotropic material on a distorted chart are the same problem, and one envelope covers both. A chart could even absorb all of a material's anisotropy (by the measurable Riemann mapping theorem), handing the network an isotropic coefficient, at the cost of one Beltrami-equation solve per piece proposal.
  • Vector unknowns in the local frame make rotations exact. Writing $u=R\tilde u$ with $R$ the chart's rotation field of angle $\vartheta$, the strain in the local frame is $\operatorname{sym}\bigl(\hat\nabla\tilde u\,U^{-1}+W\tilde u\otimes U^{-1}\hat\nabla\vartheta\bigr)$, $W$ the rotation by $90^\circ$ (Appendix B). For an isotropic material the energy depends on the chart only through $U$ and $\hat\nabla\vartheta$, so a rigidly rotated piece is the identical problem by construction, not by training.
  • The Piola transform preserves zero divergence, $\hat\nabla\cdot\hat b=J\,\nabla\cdot b$, so an incompressible flow is seen as incompressible on the square.

Slot 2: the trace space fixes the expert's shape

"What happens on the boundary" means different things for different PDEs: a value for heat conduction, a value and a slope for a plate, a tangential field for Maxwell's equations, incoming waves for a hyperbolic system. The space of admissible boundary data, the trace space, determines what the modes are.

Slots 3 to 5: ports, the host contract, backbone and time

  • Ports come from Atlas's closed vocabulary of five power bonds, each an effort and a flow whose product is power:
    • MECH: traction and velocity;
    • ROT: torque and angular velocity;
    • THERM: temperature and entropy flux $q_n/T$;
    • ELEC: potential and current density;
    • ADVEC: a mass flux carrying a declared list of passengers, the total enthalpy $h_0$ and the species fractions $Y_k$.
  • Exact conservation depends on the class:
    • G0: total flux, force and moment per piece, because the constant and rigid modes are port modes.
    • G3: every conserved quantity, through one shared flux per face.
    • Incompressible families: mass, through divergence-free modes.
  • The host contract is the class of Table 8.
  • Backbone and time. The default backbone is the U-shaped convolutional trunk of Section 4.4, with heads for modes, the predicted field, adjoint modes (G0′), dual modes (Section 10) and enrichment. Time enters as steady, implicit ($\Fo$ as a reaction coefficient), explicit (a Courant number), or exponential (Section 9).

The Expert Card

Every expert, learned or classical, ships with a manifest that the host reads before admitting it (Appendix A). It declares the family, ports, chart methods and envelope, the time range, resolution, output format, certificates and precision. The general form adds seven fields:

  • trace_space;
  • class;
  • label_map, the physical names of the generic unknowns, so one network can be several experts;
  • bond_channels, the co-located inputs it accepts (Section 6.4);
  • heads;
  • dual;
  • coupling_numbers.

The host's rule is simple: anything outside a declared range is refused with the violated quantity named, and a missing certificate is reported, never assumed.

6How different physics meet

In short

A decomposition creates eight kinds of interaction between pieces (Table 11). The three that matter most are drawn in Figure 6:

  • the same physics across a shared side, which version 1 already handles;
  • different physics across a shared side, such as the air against a rocket's skin;
  • different fields on the same piece, such as temperature and stress in one frame.

Every interaction is handled by the host, never by one network knowing about another. All of them rest on one principle, which is machine-checked: if every expert only stores or dissipates energy, and every connection only passes energy on, the assembly cannot create energy and cannot run away.

6.1The principle under all eight: passivity

The picture

A building made of springs and dampers joined by rigid links cannot start shaking by itself: every spring only stores the energy you put in, every damper only loses it, and a link only passes force and motion from one part to the next. If each piece of a simulation behaves like that, and each joint only passes power from one side to the other, the whole simulation inherits the same stability, without anyone analysing the whole.

A system is passive if its stored energy grows by no more than the power supplied at its ports [50]. Every port in Atlas is an effort–flow pair whose product is power, and two ports connect by the rule "efforts equal, flows opposite", $e_B=e_A$ and $f_B=-f_A$, so $e_Af_A+e_Bf_B=0$. Passive systems joined this way form a passive system [51].

Proposition 13 (Passivity composes.)

Under the connection rule the power entering one port equals the power leaving its partner, so over any graph of connections the junction powers sum to zero. If every agent is passive, the whole system is passive with respect to its open ports, with stored energy the sum of the agents'. If every agent is incrementally passive and two runs receive the same external forcing, one coupled step does not increase the distance between the runs. machine-checked Atlas.port_rule_power, Atlas.junction_power_eq_zero, Atlas.interconnection_passive, Atlas.interconnection_nonexpansive

Each guarantee class is one way of making a discrete expert passive:

  • G0: its Gram is positive semidefinite (Theorem 5).
  • G1: it minimises a convex energy.
  • G3: it conserves exactly, and is passive in the entropy sense only with entropy-stable fluxes.
  • G0′: it is passive only when the port pairing counts the advected energy, which is why transport seams exchange the advective flux explicitly.
  • G2: it has no structural guarantee, which is why it carries a certificate and a fallback.

6.2I-1: the same physics across a shared side

What must hold: the primal quantity is continuous (temperature, displacement) and its dual balances (heat flux, traction). How:

  • G0: the Gram superelement of Section 4.6.
  • G0′: the same with adjoint test modes.
  • G1, G2: minimise the total energy or residual over the shared port coordinates.
  • G3: the side is a face, and the host computes one numerical flux from the two one-sided states, a Riemann solver: the flux that results from the jump between two constant states. Both pieces use that flux, so conservation is exact. At open boundaries, ports carry characteristic variables: what flows in is data, what flows out is computed [52, 53].

Corners where three or more pieces meet need vertex continuity. Plates and stream functions carry extra corner conditions: a concentrated corner force from the twisting moment, and compatible slopes from two sides. Floating pieces (no prescribed boundary) have singular stiffness, constants for heat and rigid motions for elasticity. The assembled system is still nonsingular whenever the whole problem is; in 3-D a coarse space carrying these null spaces is needed and is open.

6.3I-2: different physics across a shared side: the air against the rocket's skin

The picture

The air solves for density, velocity, pressure and temperature; the skin for displacement, stress and temperature. They need not agree on any of that. They must agree only on what crosses the skin. The first is mechanical power: the wall moves the air exactly as fast as the wall moves, and the air pushes back on the wall with the traction it feels. The second is heat: temperature is continuous, and the heat leaving one side enters the other. Think of each side of a piece as carrying plugs, one per kind of energy exchange; two pieces connect wherever their plugs match.

  1. Typed ports. A side carries a list of typed power pairs. At the rocket's skin these are MECH (traction $\mathbf t=\sigma\mathbf n$, velocity $\mathbf v$) and THERM (temperature, entropy flux $q_n/T$), plus ADVEC with its passenger list wherever mass crosses (an ablating or transpiration-cooled skin). Two experts share a side for type $P$ only if both declare $P$ there, with the same passengers. An empty intersection is a modelling statement that the host reports.
  2. One physical frame. Vectors are exchanged in the seam's own frame $(\mathbf n,\tau)$, never in either chart's coordinates; tractions transform by Nanson's formula $\mathbf n\,ds=J\,DF^{-\top}\hat{\mathbf n}\,d\hat s$.
  3. Different resolutions on the two sides. If values go from frame to air by $u_{\text{air}}=P\,u_{\text{frame}}$, fluxes must come back by the transpose, $f_{\text{frame}}=P^\top f_{\text{air}}$. Then $f_{\text{air}}\cdot u_{\text{air}}=f_{\text{frame}}\cdot u_{\text{frame}}$ identically, so the transfer creates no power, the principle behind partitioned analysis [54] derived.
  4. Who imposes what. The stiffer side receives the push and tells the softer side the position: the air is told where the wall is and how hot it is, and the skin is told the traction and heat flux the air returns.
    • Thermally this is the classical stability result for conjugate heat transfer [55], with optimal Robin coefficients in [56].
    • Mechanically the danger is the added-mass instability, which appears when the fluid is heavy compared with the structure, such as dense propellant against a thin tank wall [57]. Robin exchange [58] and added-mass partitioned schemes [59] are the cures.
    • In the project's wing case the structure's interface stiffness is about 5300 times the fluid's in every mode, safely one-sided measured.
  5. The chart operator's own instrument: condensed impedance. A linear skin's Gram is its exact boundary stiffness. The host can therefore hand it to the air as a boundary condition, so that the air solves against the skin's exact response instead of exchanging guesses with it. For an implicitly stepped structure that stiffness, $V^\top(K+\tfrac{1}{\beta\Delta t^2}M)V$, includes the structure's inertia, so the coupled solve is monolithic in port space and the added-mass instability cannot arise derived, not measured.
  6. Imperfect interfaces. Some seams obey a law of their own:
    • contact resistance, $q_n=h_c(T_1-T_2)$;
    • slip at a porous wall [60];
    • friction and unilateral contact;
    • electrode kinetics;
    • an ablating surface's energy balance.
    Each is a zero-thickness element between the two ports with its own dissipation law, and it keeps passivity when the power it absorbs is non-negative.
  7. Different dimensions. A shell skin meets a solid ring frame through a kinematic constraint through the thickness; a field meets a lumped node (a coolant reservoir, a motor shaft) only through its constant or rigid port modes.
  8. Moving seams. Small motion is imposed as a "transpiration" velocity on the fixed geometry. Large motion re-charts the fluid piece every step, gives the expert the mesh velocity, and enforces the geometric conservation law: a uniform flow must stay exactly uniform on a moving grid [61].
  9. Different time steps. The air steps in milliseconds and the skin's heating in seconds. The slow side receives the time integral of the fast side's flux over its step, so energy is transferred exactly.

Either side may also be a classical solver exposing the same ports, which is how a family enters Atlas before its network exists.

6.4I-3: different fields on one piece: temperature and stress in one frame

The picture

Suppose we have a trained temperature expert and a trained stress expert, but nothing trained on both. Both sit on the same chart, the same sheet of graph paper, so a field from one is already on the other's grid: no interpolation, no seam. Neither expert knows the other exists. The host owns the coupling law, exactly as it owns boundary conditions, and translates each expert's output into the other's ordinary inputs. A temperature becomes a thermal-expansion load on the stress expert, and a strain rate becomes a heat source on the temperature expert. Then the host solves the coupled problem in the space the two experts' fields span.

The structure. One implicit step of linear thermoelasticity ($\theta=T-T_0$), with $K_u$ the stiffness, $G$ the thermal-load operator, and the energy equation scaled by $-\Delta t/T_0$, reads

\begin{equation}\label{eq:thermoelastic} \begin{pmatrix}K_u&-G\\-G^\top&-N\end{pmatrix}\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf u\\\theta\end{pmatrix}=\begin{pmatrix}\mathbf f\\ \mathbf r\end{pmatrix},\qquad N=\frac{C_\theta+\Delta t\,K_\theta}{T_0}\succ0 . \end{equation}

This matrix is symmetric quasi-definite: a positive definite block, a negative definite block, and a coupling that appears with its transpose. Such matrices are always nonsingular [62]. Define the dimensionless coupling strength $\gamma=\lVert N^{-1/2}G^\top K_u^{-1/2}\rVert_2$.

Proposition 14 (Two experts, one piece.)

For any trial spaces $\tilde U$ from the stress expert and $\tilde\Theta$ from the temperature expert:

  1. the host's reduced matrix has exactly $\dim\tilde U$ positive and $\dim\tilde\Theta$ negative eigenvalues, all of modulus in $[1,\sqrt{1+\gamma^2}]$ once each basis is energy-orthonormalised, so its condition number is at most $\sqrt{1+\gamma^2}$;
  2. the Galerkin solution is within $1+\gamma$ of the best approximation in $\tilde U\times\tilde\Theta$, in the block energy norm;
  3. alternating between the two experts (a block Gauss–Seidel sweep) contracts the error by $\gamma^2$ per sweep;
  4. $\gamma^2\le\delta=\beta^2T_0/\bigl((\lambda+2\mu)\rho c\bigr)$, the classical thermoelastic coupling number, about 0.01 for metals.

In plain words: for metals the coupled problem is almost as well conditioned as two separate ones, whatever the experts return. derived measured

Show the proof sketch

(1) In energy-orthonormal bases the reduced matrix is $\bigl(\begin{smallmatrix}I&C^\top\\C&-I\end{smallmatrix}\bigr)$ with $\lVert C\rVert\le\gamma$; its square is $\operatorname{diag}(I+C^\top C,\,I+CC^\top)$. (2) The form is $T$-coercive with $T=\operatorname{diag}(I,-I)$, which maps the product trial space to itself [63]; its inf-sup constant is 1 and its continuity constant $1+\gamma$, and the sharpened Babuška estimate [64] gives $1+\gamma$. (3) The iteration matrix has spectral radius $\rho(N^{-1}G^\top K_u^{-1}G)=\gamma^2$. (4) Fourier analysis of the continuum operators: the bound is attained at long wavelengths, where conduction is negligible over one step. ∎

Three levels of resolving a bond, measured on a steel bar with $\gamma^2=\delta=0.0108$ (arch_general_form_checks.py):

Cross-enrichment. The two experts' uncoupled answers cannot represent the coupled solution: with a purely thermal load, the stress expert's own answer is zero and the thermal expansion is missing entirely. So each round asks each expert for its response to the other's latest field, through its source input, and adds it to the trial space. Each round gains a factor $\gamma^2$. With exact experts the displacement error fell from 100% to $2.8\times10^{-3}$ to $8.5\times10^{-6}$ over rounds 0, 1 and 2 measured. On the unit square, the plain square's own low sine modes could serve as free generic enrichment without any expert call proposal.

The other co-located cases.

  • Strong reciprocal coupling (soft porous media, piezoelectrics). Move the coupling's diagonal part into one expert's coefficient input. This is the fixed-stress split of poroelasticity, unconditionally stable [65] and a proven contraction [66], and the adiabatic split of thermoelasticity [67]. In both, the flow or mechanics expert just receives a modified coefficient, so no new expert is needed if its envelope covers it proposal.
  • Dissipative coupling (Joule heating with temperature-dependent conductivity, frictional heating) is not symmetric, and is handled by Newton's method on the product trial space. Power bookkeeping is exact: the Joule heat handed to the thermal expert integrates to exactly the electrical power at the ports, $\int\sigma\lvert\nabla\phi\rvert^2=c^\top\tilde\Lambda_\sigma c$, because both come from the same projected field derived.
  • Stiff pointwise chemistry uses three ingredients:
    • Strang splitting, half a chemistry step, a transport step, half a chemistry step, which is second order in $\Delta t$ [68];
    • a small chemistry expert evaluated at every grid point;
    • an exact projection afterwards onto element conservation $\{Y:EY=EY^0\}$ ($E$ the element–species matrix), then positivity.
  • The coupling envelope. Every bond declares its coupling number in advance (thermoelastic $\delta$, Biot ratio, electromechanical factor). If $\gamma^2$ is below the tolerance, one-way suffices; if $\gamma\lt1$, staggered or monolithic; if $\gamma\ge1$, only monolithic, because alternation diverges.
  • Where it ends. Phenomena of the joint nonlinearity, such as thermal buckling, a bifurcation of the coupled system, are not reachable by two separate experts. The host reports the loss of definiteness as an instability, not as an error.

6.5I-4 to I-8, briefly

  • I-4, non-local exchange.
    • Surface radiation between distant surfaces couples sides that never touch. The net exchange is symmetric in the emissive powers $\sigma T^4$ (by reciprocity of view factors) but not in $T$, so it is G2: Newton's method on the radiating sides' port coordinates, with the exchange applied as a dense or hierarchical matrix.
    • Exterior acoustic or electrostatic fields need an exterior Dirichlet-to-Neumann map, or a ring of absorbing pieces, which are complex charts (Section 7).
  • I-5, global constraints and lumped parts. The pressure level of an enclosed flow, the potential of an isolated conductor, the mass in a closed coolant loop and the vehicle's rigid-body motion each add one host unknown, coupled to every participating piece through one mode. Lumped components (circuits, pipe networks, motors, batteries) are port-Hamiltonian ODEs attached the same way.
  • I-6, changing geometry or topology. A changed shape is re-charted and re-certified, and cut again if it leaves the envelope; the new stiffness costs one forward pass. A changed topology (a stage separating, a gap opening) is a new decomposition, with the state transferred by conservative projection. Topology belongs to the decomposition, never to a network.
  • I-7, several time scales. Multirate stepping with time-integrated fluxes; waveform relaxation (each piece integrates a whole window given its neighbours' histories, iterated); quasi-static elimination of fast subsystems.
  • I-8, several length scales. A microstructure is replaced by effective coefficients from a periodic cell problem, $e\cdot\kappa^*e=\min_\chi\int_Y\kappa\lvert e+\nabla\chi\rvert^2$. Because this is an energy, a chart operator on the cell gives an upper bound for any trial space, and in 2-D the dual expert of Section 10 gives a lower bound [69, 70]. In FE$^2$ [71] the chart operator would be the micro-solver; the classical fast solver on a periodic cell is FFT-based [72].

6.6Seams, bonds and the rocket, collected

The rocket, through this lens. The project's rocket case study has seven agents: combustion reaction $a$, chamber $b$, airframe $c$, atmosphere-front $d$, outflow $e$, plume $f$, atmosphere-wake $g$. In a chart-operator decomposition they map as follows.

  • The walls $b$–$c$ and $c$–$d$ are I-2 seams.
  • Inside the airframe $c$ sits the thermoelastic bond.
  • The gas–gas edges are I-1 with Riemann fluxes.
  • The plume radiating to the base is I-4; the trajectory and gravity are I-5; nozzle recession and staging are I-6.

In short

Table 15 lists about forty PDEs the experts will cover, grouped by the family that serves each, with its guarantee class and its use in Atlas's case studies (the rocket ascent, the wind farm wake, and the vehicle ladder CS-9 to CS-16). The catalog is small because one network can serve many PDEs: experts are separated by trace space and class, and an expert used on a neighbouring PDE with the same trace space only costs accuracy.

Six observations that keep the catalog small derived:

  1. Same trace space and class, same network. Theorem 5's identity holds for any interior field with the right trace. An expert trained on PDE $X$ but used on PDE $Y$ therefore gives $\tilde\Lambda-\Lambda_Y=E^\top A_YE$ with $E=H_X-H_Y$: consistent, and quadratically less accurate. The Expert Card's label_map renames the unknowns.
  2. The Kirchhoff transform moves nonlinearity onto the seams. In one material with $\kappa=\kappa(T)$, the variable $\Theta=\int_{T_0}^{T}\kappa(s)\,ds$ satisfies $\kappa(T)\nabla T=\nabla\Theta$. The steady piece problem is then linear in $\Theta$ and uses the linear expert's modes unchanged. Since the layout cuts at material interfaces, every piece is one material, and the nonlinearity sits only in the seam condition $K_1^{-1}(\Theta_1)=K_2^{-1}(\Theta_2)$, solved by Newton's method on a few hundred port coordinates.
  3. One biharmonic network, several uses. The isotropic plate $D\nabla^4w=q$ and 2-D Stokes flow in stream-function form are the same equation, with clamped edges corresponding to no-slip walls.
  4. Implicit acoustics and implicit heat are both $-\nabla\cdot(\mathsf A\nabla u)+c\,u$ with $c\gt0$.
  5. An absorbing layer is a complex chart. A perfectly matched layer stretches the coordinate by $s_x=1+i\sigma(x)/\omega$. Pulled back, it is exactly \eqref{eq:pullback} with a complex Jacobian: $\mathsf A=\operatorname{diag}(s_y/s_x,\,s_x/s_y)$ and $J=s_xs_y$. A Helmholtz expert with complex coefficient inputs handles absorbing pieces with no special code.
  6. In two dimensions the dual of the diffusion expert is the diffusion expert, run on the reciprocal conductivity (Section 10).

9Speed from the square

In short

Because every piece is the same square on the same grid, three things become available that an unstructured mesh does not offer:

  1. all pieces stack into one array for a single batched network call;
  2. the plain square's operator is diagonalised exactly by fast sine and cosine transforms;
  3. every hierarchy (multigrid, quadtrees, wavelets) is the trivial halving one.

The three biggest measured consequences: a preconditioned solve needs the same 30 iterations at every resolution; high port modes can be computed only near their side; and a low-precision network costs almost nothing in accuracy.

The square's eight symmetries can also be built into the network [91], which buys exactness and data efficiency but not speed.

9.1S2: the plain square's fast solve as a preconditioner

The picture

The plain square's Laplacian can be inverted almost for free with a fast sine transform. A distorted piece is "the Laplacian, bent and rescaled", so the plain inverse is an excellent approximate inverse for it. Conjugate gradients then needs a number of iterations set by how bent and how heterogeneous the piece is, and not by how fine the grid is.

Pointwise, $\mathsf A=\kappa JG^{-1}$ has eigenvalues $\kappa K$ and $\kappa/K$. For conforming discretisations this gives $aL\preceq A\preceq bL$, with $L$ the plain Laplacian, and

\begin{equation}\label{eq:pcg} \frac ba\le\frac{\kappa_{\max}}{\kappa_{\min}}K_{\max}^2,\qquad \text{iterations}\le\Bigl\lceil\tfrac12\sqrt{b/a}\,\ln(2/\mathrm{tol})\Bigr\rceil, \end{equation}

the standard conjugate-gradient bound, independent of the grid. Each iteration costs one fast transform pair and one sparse product.

The measured spectral ratio at $32^2$ was 23.1, so the bound is about four times pessimistic. This gives a classical fallback whose cost is guaranteed and does not grow with resolution. It also suggests an alternative output form, $V=V_0+L_0^{-1}N_\theta$: the network emits a source-like field and the fast solve turns it into a correction with exact zero trace proposal.

9.2S7: port modes live near their side

The picture

A wiggle of wavenumber $k$ imposed on one side of a square dies out within about $1/(k\pi)$ of that side, like ripples that fade quickly away from a vibrating edge. High port modes are therefore zero over most of the square, and computing them there is wasted work.

The extension of $\sin(k\pi x)$ from $y=0$ is $\sin(k\pi x)\sinh(k\pi(1-y))/\sinh(k\pi)\approx\sin(k\pi x)e^{-k\pi y}$. An implicit step at Fourier number $\Fo$ raises the decay rate to $\sqrt{(k\pi)^2+1/\Fo}$, and distortion $K$ can lower it to $k\pi/K$. A mode is below an amplitude tolerance beyond depth $\min(1,\ln(1/\mathrm{tol})/\text{rate})$, and the measured depths matched this to within one grid cell.

Because energy is quadratic in amplitude, an amplitude tolerance of $10^{-3}$ costs a stiffness error of order $5\times10^{-6}$. The saving collapses on distorted pieces, so the envelope should set the strip depths per piece; transient pieces gain the most.

9.3S9: why a low-precision network is nearly free

The picture

Theorem 5 does not care where an error in a mode comes from. Rounding the network's output to fewer digits is just another interior error, so it enters the stiffness squared. Two conditions apply: the host must do its own arithmetic in full precision, and the rounded quantity should be small. The principal part makes it small, since the network only outputs a correction to the plain square's exact modes.

The identity held to $7\times10^{-16}$ and the error stayed positive semidefinite. The policy that follows: the network may compute in bfloat16, a 16-bit format standard on accelerators, and the host forms every Gram in double precision proposal. The G2 and G3 classes have no quadratic identity, so for them rounding costs linearly.

10A two-sided certificate from two-dimensional duality

In short

The value-based expert can only over-estimate a piece's stiffness (Theorem 5). A flux-based "dual" expert can only under-estimate it. The truth lies in between, so the gap between the two is a computable, guaranteed bound on the error of both. In two dimensions the flux problem is a diffusion problem with the conductivity inverted, so the same network computes it. Measured: for smooth boundary data the gap was within a factor of 3 of the true error.

For boundary data $g$ the port energy is $\mathcal E(g)=\min\{\int_\Omega\mathsf A\nabla v\cdot\nabla v:\ v|_{\partial\Omega}=g\}$. For every divergence-free flux $\tau$, the divergence theorem and Young's inequality give

\begin{equation}\label{eq:dual} 2\oint_{\partial\Omega}g\,\tau\cdot n\,ds-\int_\Omega\tau\cdot\mathsf A^{-1}\tau\,dx\;\le\;\mathcal E(g), \end{equation}

with equality at the true flux. This is the two-energy argument of Prager and Synge [92]. In 2-D every divergence-free field on a simply connected piece is $\tau=W\nabla\psi$, with $W$ the rotation by $90^\circ$ and $\psi$ a stream function. Then $\tau\cdot n=-\partial_t\psi$ along the boundary and $W^\top\mathsf A^{-1}W=\mathsf A/\det\mathsf A$. For the pull-back of an isotropic $\kappa$, $\mathsf A/\det\mathsf A=\kappa^{-1}JG^{-1}$: the same Beltrami coefficient with the conductivity inverted, the two-dimensional reciprocity of conductivities [69, 70]. So the dual expert is the CO-D network with input $\log\kappa\mapsto-\log\kappa$, reading its port data as stream-function boundary values.

Proposition 15 (The dual bracket.)

Let $Q_*$ be a basis of continuous dual traces, $\Psi$ the dual expert's modes and $Y=Q_*^\top TQ$ the boundary pairing, and let the host form $\tilde\Lambda_{\text{low}}=Y^\top(\tilde\Psi^\top M_*\tilde\Psi)^{-1}Y$. For conforming discretisations of both problems and every output of both networks,

\begin{equation}\label{eq:bracket} \tilde\Lambda_{\text{low}}\preceq\Lambda_{\text{low}}\preceq\mathcal E\preceq\Lambda\preceq\tilde\Lambda, \end{equation}

so the computable gap $\tilde\Lambda-\tilde\Lambda_{\text{low}}$ dominates the true error $\tilde\Lambda-\mathcal E$, discretisation included. derived measured

Show why

The middle inequalities are \eqref{eq:dual} and the Dirichlet principle restricted to conforming subspaces. The outer ones are Theorem 5(ii) applied to each expert: the dual Gram over-estimates its exact value, $\tilde G_*-G_*=E_*^\top A_{*,II}E_*\succeq0$, and inversion reverses the Loewner order on positive definite matrices, so $Y^\top\tilde G_*^{-1}Y\preceq Y^\top G_*^{-1}Y$. ∎

Why it matters
  • A guaranteed error bar for every 2-D diffusion piece, for one more batched forward pass and a small dense solve.
  • It needs continuous dual traces. A stream function's boundary values are continuous with kinks at the corners, which cosines that jump at corners cannot represent. That is a second reason, after Remark 4, for the conforming port basis.
  • Worst-case boundary data need about twice the primal's port count on the dual side, but the smooth data an actual solve produces do not.
  • On the paper's own finite volumes the bracket's floor should vanish. Those finite volumes form a planar resistor network, and planar duality should make the discrete bracket exact, so the gap would measure the networks' errors alone open, unchecked.

11Training

In short

Training examples come from the project's own classical solver, so they are unlimited, licence-free and in exactly the right format. Better still, the Gram identity gives a training objective that needs no solved examples at all: minimising the energy of the network's own fields is provably the same as supervised training in the energy norm (Corollary 16). Training proceeds family by family, each stage gated. The whole two-dimensional catalog is estimated at roughly 100–300 GPU-hours, about \$70–200 at the project's rental cap estimate.

Data. An example is a random piece with its chart, coefficient and source: random closed spline curves cut into four sides, charted by the rule of Table 4, binned by $\sup|\mu|$ and the range of $\log J$ so that coverage of the envelope can be measured. Log-conductivities and sources are smooth random fields with material interfaces along random curves, and $\Fo$ is drawn log-uniformly from the declared range. Exact labels cost one sparse factorisation and $4m+1$ pairs of triangular solves per example.

Objective. Errors are measured in the energy norm of each example's own problem, the norm that controls the stiffness error by Theorem 5(ii):

\begin{equation}\label{eq:loss} \mathcal L_{\text{sup}}(\theta)=\mathbb E\Bigl[\ \sum_{k=1}^{4m}\lVert\hat H_k(\theta)-H_k\rVert_{A_I}^2+\lVert\hat u_p(\theta)-u_p\rVert_{A_I}^2\Bigr], \end{equation}

averaged over examples and the square's eight symmetries.

Corollary 16 (A training objective with no solved examples.)

For one example let $\mathcal L_{\text{en}}(\theta)=\tr\tilde\Lambda(\theta)+\hat u_p(\theta)^\top A_I\,\hat u_p(\theta)-2f^\top\hat u_p(\theta)$, which needs only the network's output and the piece's energy matrix. Then $\mathcal L_{\text{en}}$ and the summand of \eqref{eq:loss} differ by a constant independent of $\theta$, so they have the same gradients and the same minimisers.

In plain words: train the network to make its own fields as low-energy as possible. Because the true fields are the lowest-energy ones with the given boundary values, this is exactly training towards them, without ever computing them. machine-checked Atlas.Piece.labelFree_eq_supervised_add_const

Show the proof

By Theorem 5(ii), $\tr\tilde\Lambda=\tr\Lambda+\sum_k\lVert\hat H_k-H_k\rVert_{A_I}^2$. Since $u_p=A_I^{-1}f$ minimises $v\mapsto v^\top A_Iv-2f^\top v$, completing the square gives $\hat u_p^\top A_I\hat u_p-2f^\top\hat u_p=-u_p^\top A_Iu_p+\lVert\hat u_p-u_p\rVert_{A_I}^2$. Neither $\tr\Lambda$ nor $u_p^\top A_Iu_p$ depends on $\theta$. ∎

This is the discrete Ritz principle, as in the Deep Ritz method [93], here provably equal to supervised training. Labelled examples are still needed for validation, because the objective's value contains the unknown constant. In floating point the two objectives are not identical (the label-free gradient involves a cancelling difference), so which trains better at equal cost is a registered comparison. For networks that approximate the exact evolution over a time interval, a penalty enforcing "one step of $2\Delta t$ equals two steps of $\Delta t$" is added; the implicit-step experts do not have this property and do not get the penalty.

Training from scratch or fine-tuning. Stages 0 and 1 train small networks from scratch: the physics is linear or nearly so, examples are cheap, and small networks are what the cost requirement S7 demands. A chart maps every piece onto the uniform grid that pretrained models accept, which makes fine-tuning them possible for the first time here. So Stage 2 compares, at equal compute:

  1. a network trained from scratch;
  2. a pretrained model (DPOT [26], Walrus [27] or GPhyT [28]) fine-tuned by low-rank adaptation [29] with new input layers;
  3. model 2 compressed into the size of model 1.

Poseidon is excluded by its licence (row S12).

The largest uncertainty is whether $10^5$ samples and 100 epochs reach the accuracy gate E1 at a useful distortion range. That is unknowable before a run, and ten times more data or epochs scales every figure linearly open.

12Cost: when a learned superelement pays

In short

The decomposition with condensed pieces needs no rounds of iteration, where classical coupling needs tens to thousands. But in two dimensions, at the sizes measured, one direct solve of the whole problem is faster than every decomposed method, and stored classical modes beat any network on a fixed geometry. A learned superelement pays where classical work cannot be reused or does not scale: when shape, material or parameters change between solves (design optimisation, uncertainty studies), when the local physics is expensive, and in three dimensions.

All timings below come from one cloud container with four cores, so only ratios are reported. The coupling probe arch_coupling_cost.py solves the problem charts produce, square pieces of $n\times n$ cells with a smooth random conductivity, with exact classical piece solves standing in for networks. That is the best case for any learned solver: no network converges in fewer rounds than its exact counterpart.

On $8\times8$ pieces the counts were 1,864 rounds for restricted additive Schwarz, 1,124 for Robin exchange, 152 with Anderson acceleration, 83 with GMRES, 70 for the two-level method, and none for the superelement measured. Wall times tell a different story (Table 24): a direct solve of the whole problem was faster than every decomposed method.

The arithmetic of when it pays
  • Per call, the network is dearer. One forward pass of a 1–2 million parameter network costs 8 to 17 ms per piece at $n=64$, against 0.85 ms for a classical piece solve with a stored factorisation: 9 to 20 times more expensive per call.
  • The advantage is the number of calls. A condensed solve needs one pass per piece, 64 calls on $8\times8$ pieces, against $70\times64=4{,}480$ calls for the two-level method and about $1.2\times10^5$ for restricted additive Schwarz.
  • Against the classical superelement, it depends on reuse. Recomputing one port matrix classically takes about 68 ms, so the network is 4 to 9 times cheaper. When nothing changes, though, classical modes are computed once and then cost nothing, and no network can compete.
  • A whole solve. On $8\times8$ pieces a condensed solve with the medium network would take about 0.2–0.4 s, against 0.8 s for the direct solve estimate.

13How the results will be judged

In short

The pass/fail criteria are written into code with their numerical bars before any training run, so results cannot shape them. A result that fails a gate is reported as a failure. Benchmark geometries (the s-channel, the three-armed bend, a block with a round insert) are kept out of the training data.

14Related work, and what is new

In short

Learned piece solvers composed by iteration exist, and so do learned boundary maps, reduced models and geometry-aware operators. Not found in the works read: a trial-space generator reusable across geometries through certified charts, with exact traces, typed multi-physics ports, and a guarantee class declared from the physics and enforced by the host, with the linear core machine-checked.

  • Learned piece solvers in Schwarz iterations. Schwarz Neural Inference [35] composes a transformer by overlapping Schwarz iteration; its convergence theorem is discussed in Remark 10. Other examples:
    • neural-Schwarz tiling [94], whose iteration count grows from 4 to 61 with resolution without a coarse space;
    • a learning-based decomposition method on affine squares [95];
    • mosaic flows [96];
    • Robin-exchange methods [97, 98];
    • a learned preconditioner in a two-level method, about twice as slow as a parallel direct solver [99];
    • a finite-element/neural-operator hybrid with iteration counts comparable to the classical coupling [100].
    All exchange values or Robin data and iterate. None returns constraint modes or forms the stiffness from the network's fields.
  • Learned parts of decomposition methods:
    • learned Robin interface matrices [101];
    • neural operators interleaved with relaxation sweeps [102];
    • a survey [103].
  • Geometry:
    • diffeomorphic transport to a reference domain [104, 105];
    • learned deformations without an injectivity guarantee [32];
    • operators on point clouds and meshes [106, 107];
    • a benchmark of topological generalisation [33].
    None passes each piece's pulled-back coefficient to the network.
  • Learned boundary maps and local stiffness:
    • learned local Dirichlet-to-Neumann maps of nonlinear problems [108];
    • principal part plus learned remainder [109];

15Limitations and open questions

  1. Nothing has been trained. Whether the general input class can be learned to useful accuracy at a forward cost below the classical alternatives is unknown open.
  2. Cost in 2-D. A direct solve of the whole problem beats every decomposed method at the sizes measured, and stored classical modes beat any network on a fixed geometry. Gate E5 may fail.
  3. Three dimensions. Harmonic charts carry no injectivity theorem there, cutting a solid into topological cubes is an open meshing problem, and the 3-D interface solver (preconditioned CG with a coarse space) is untested.
  4. Classes G2 and G3 rest on cited results that nothing here checks; shocks that cross a seam are outside scope.
  5. Non-matching side parametrisations make the method nonconforming, and the optimality theorem does not cover them.
  6. The general-form statements (Petrov–Galerkin identity, G1 bounds, the two-experts proposition, the dual bracket) are derived and checked numerically but not yet machine-checked; the thermoelastic check is one-dimensional.
  7. The block layout that cuts an arbitrary domain into four-sided blocks is not built.
  8. All measurements come from one container, not the owner's laptop, and the requirements S1–S12 from one network.

Host rules.

  • A chart outside the envelope, or a Fourier number outside the declared range, is refused, and the violated quantity is named.
  • G0 is admitted for constraint-mode output on linear symmetric physics, with no further certificate.
  • A black-box convergence claim needs a certified contraction factor below one.
  • A bond must declare its coupling number.
  • A missing certificate is reported, never assumed.

BDerivations

The metric in terms of the Beltrami coefficient. With $F_\xi=F_z+F_{\bar z}$, $F_\eta=i(F_z-F_{\bar z})$ and $\mu=F_{\bar z}/F_z$: $|F_\xi|^2=|F_z|^2|1+\mu|^2$, $|F_\eta|^2=|F_z|^2|1-\mu|^2$, and $\langle F_\xi,F_\eta\rangle=2|F_z|^2\operatorname{Im}\mu$. The identity $|1+\mu|^2|1-\mu|^2-4(\operatorname{Im}\mu)^2=(1-|\mu|^2)^2$ gives $\det G=J^2$ with $J=|F_z|^2(1-|\mu|^2)$; inverting $G$ and multiplying by $J$ gives \eqref{eq:metric-mu}.

Proof of Proposition 2. Let $L=DF$ at the corner, with $Le_1=a\,u_1$ and $Le_2=b\,u_2$ for unit vectors $u_1,u_2$ enclosing the angle $\alpha$. The quadrant maps onto the convex cone they span, so $\alpha\lt\pi$. Then

$$K+\frac1K=\frac{\tr(L^\top L)}{\sqrt{\det L^\top L}}=\frac{a^2+b^2}{ab\sin\alpha}\ge\frac{2}{\sin\alpha},$$

so $K\ge(1+|\cos\alpha|)/\sin\alpha$, which is $\cot(\alpha/2)$ for $\alpha\le\pi/2$ and $\tan(\alpha/2)$ for $\alpha\ge\pi/2$. ∎

The conformal chart at a corner. Near a corner of angle $\alpha$ a conformal chart behaves like $w=\zeta^{2\alpha/\pi}$, so $J=|dw/d\zeta|^2\propto r^{2-\pi/\alpha}$ with $r=|w|$.

The principal part on the square. For boundary values $\cos(k\pi\xi)$ on the bottom side and zero elsewhere, the steady principal part is

\begin{equation}\label{eq:principal} H_k^{(0)}(\xi,\eta)=\sum_{j\ge1}c_{jk}\,\sin(j\pi\xi)\,\frac{\sinh\bigl(\omega_j(1-\eta)\bigr)}{\sinh\omega_j},\qquad c_{jk}=\frac{2j\,\bigl(1-(-1)^{j+k}\bigr)}{\pi\,(j^2-k^2)}\ (j\ne k),\quad c_{kk}=0, \end{equation}

with $\omega_j=j\pi$; for one implicit step, $\omega_j=\sqrt{j^2\pi^2+\Fo^{-1}}$. The networks use the exact discrete counterpart, computed by a fast sine transform.

The strain in the local frame. With $u=R\tilde u$, $R=R(\vartheta)$ and $R'=RW$: $\nabla_xu=R\bigl(W\tilde u\otimes\nabla_x\vartheta+\nabla_x\tilde u\bigr)$. Using $DF^{-1}R=U^{-1}$ and $R^\top\nabla_x\vartheta=U^{-1}\hat\nabla\vartheta$, the strain written in the local frame is $R^\top\varepsilon R=\operatorname{sym}\bigl(\hat\nabla\tilde u\,U^{-1}+W\tilde u\otimes U^{-1}\hat\nabla\vartheta\bigr)$. For an isotropic material the energy is invariant under $\varepsilon\mapsto R^\top\varepsilon R$, so it depends on the chart only through $U$ and $\hat\nabla\vartheta$.

Absorbing layers as complex charts. With $DF=\operatorname{diag}(s_x,s_y)$, $s_x=1+i\sigma_x/\omega$: $J=s_xs_y$ and $J\,DF^{-1}DF^{-\top}=\operatorname{diag}(s_y/s_x,s_x/s_y)$, the standard anisotropic form of a perfectly matched layer.

Two-dimensional duality of the coefficient. For a symmetric $2\times2$ matrix $\mathsf A$ and $W$ the rotation by $90^\circ$, $W^\top\mathsf A^{-1}W=\mathsf A/\det\mathsf A$; for $\mathsf A=\kappa JG^{-1}$ with $\det(JG^{-1})=1$, $\det\mathsf A=\kappa^2$, so $\mathsf A/\det\mathsf A=\kappa^{-1}JG^{-1}$.

The Kirchhoff transform. With $\Theta=\int_{T_0}^T\kappa(s)\,ds$, $\nabla\Theta=\kappa(T)\nabla T$, so $-\nabla\cdot(\kappa(T)\nabla T)=f$ becomes $-\Delta\Theta=f$ in each single-material piece.

CReproducibility

Every measurement is produced by a script in the repository, run from its root with python scripts/<name>.py after setting PYTHONIOENCODING=utf-8. The scripts need NumPy and SciPy; the network timing also needs PyTorch. None trains a network or downloads data.

DGlossary

adjoint mode
A field describing how a boundary measurement depends on the interior; needed as a test field when the physics is not symmetric.
affine trial set
All fields of the form "predicted field plus a combination of modes"; the set the host searches.
Beltrami coefficient $\mu$, dilatation $K$
A complex number at each point saying how much and in which direction a map squashes tiny circles into ellipses; $K$ is the ellipse's axis ratio.
bond (co-located)
A host-owned coupling law between two fields that share a piece, such as thermal expansion between temperature and stress.
certificate
A computed, checkable quantity that bounds an error or confirms a property, such as the chart test, a residual or the dual gap.
chart
A certified map from the unit square onto a piece.
conformal map
A map that preserves angles ($\mu=0$).
constraint mode
The field inside a piece whose boundary values are one port mode and whose interior solves the source-free problem.
contraction factor
The factor by which one round of an iteration shrinks distances; below one means guaranteed convergence.
Dirichlet principle
Among all fields with given boundary values, the solution has the least energy.
Dirichlet-to-Neumann map
The map from a piece's boundary values to the flux through its boundary: its stiffness as seen from outside.
domain decomposition
Solving a PDE by cutting the domain into pieces, solving each, and enforcing agreement where they meet.
energy norm
The size of a field measured by its energy, $\lVert v\rVert_a=a(v,v)^{1/2}$.
enrichment mode
An extra interior field with zero boundary values, added to a trial space.
envelope
The declared range of inputs on which an expert is trusted; the host refuses anything outside it.
Expert Card
The manifest every expert ships with, read by the host before admitting it.
forward pass
One evaluation of a network.
Fourier number
The time step in units of a piece's diffusion time, $\kappa\Delta t/(cL^2)$.
Gram matrix
The matrix of energies of a set of fields against each other, $V^\top MV$; always positive semidefinite.
guarantee class
G0, G0′, G1, G2 or G3: what the host can guarantee for any network output, set by the structure of the physics.
host
The coupling software that owns the physics, the assembly and every law that holds across pieces.
Loewner order
$X\preceq Y$ when $Y-X$ is positive semidefinite.
LSPG
Least-squares Petrov–Galerkin: choose the trial field whose true residual is smallest.
mortar
Coupling of two differently parametrised sides through an $L^2$ projection on the seam.
neural operator
A neural network that maps fields to fields on a grid.
passive
Stored energy grows by at most the power supplied at the ports.
Péclet number
Advection against diffusion across a length, $|b|L/D$.
Piola transform
The pull-back of a flux that preserves the flux through every surface.
port, port mode, port matrix
A typed effort–flow pair on a side; one of the boundary patterns (16 per side); a piece's stiffness restricted to port modes.
principal part
The exact mode of the plain square, to which the network adds a correction.
quasi-definite matrix
A symmetric block matrix with a positive definite and a negative definite diagonal block; always invertible.
round
One solve, or one network call, on every piece.
Schur complement
The matrix that remains after eliminating interior unknowns; the discrete boundary stiffness.
superelement
A piece represented only by its boundary stiffness and load, assembled like one finite element.
trace, trace space
A field's boundary values, and the space of boundary data a PDE admits.
transmission conditions
The conditions at a shared side: the primal quantity agrees and its flux balances.
U-shaped network
A network that processes its input at successively coarser and then finer resolutions, with shortcuts.
